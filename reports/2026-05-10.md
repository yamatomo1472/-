## Second Story: The AI Tools Reshaping How Professionals Work in 2026

Artificial intelligence moved from experiment to everyday tool faster than most people expected. In 2026, the question is no longer whether AI will change your job — it already has started. The most widely used AI tools today fall into a few clear categories. For business professionals, the most immediately useful tools are those that handle text and communication. Imagine starting your workday with fifty emails in your inbox. An AI assistant can scan them, rank them by urgency, draft replies to the simple ones, and flag the two that actually need your attention. That is not a future scenario — it is something available in tools like Microsoft Copilot, which is integrated into Office 365 and widely used in Japanese corporations. Early adopters at companies like Fujitsu, NTT, and Softbank reported saving one to two hours per day using AI assistants for email and document work.

Translation and cross-language communication is another area where AI is making a real difference for Japanese professionals. DeepL, the AI translation service, has continued to improve its Japanese-English accuracy. Combined with large language models, professionals can now draft a document in Japanese, have it translated and polished in English, and review it for tone and accuracy — all in under ten minutes. This removes a major bottleneck for teams that need to communicate with overseas partners or write reports for international audiences. Several major Japanese firms have adopted this kind of workflow into their standard operating procedures.

Data analysis has also been transformed. Tools like Microsoft Excel's Copilot integration and standalone products like Julius AI allow non-technical users to upload a spreadsheet, ask questions in plain language, and receive charts, summaries, and insights within seconds. A sales manager can ask "Which region had the biggest drop in revenue last quarter?" and get a clear answer without writing a single formula. This democratizes data analysis, meaning that professionals who understand business questions can now get answers that previously required a specialist.

AI tools are also changing how presentations and reports are created. Canva's AI features, Gamma, and Microsoft Copilot for PowerPoint can take a simple outline and produce a polished deck with design, images, and structured content. This does not replace strategic thinking — you still need to know what message you are trying to send. But the time spent on formatting and visual design has dropped dramatically. Teams that used to spend a full day preparing a board presentation can now do the same work in half the time.

One important caveat applies to all of these tools: the quality of what comes out depends heavily on the quality of what goes in. Professionals who give vague, lazy instructions get vague, unreliable results. Those who learn to write clear, specific prompts — a skill sometimes called prompt engineering — consistently get much better outputs. This is a learnable skill, and it is one of the most practical things you can invest time in right now. There are free courses on platforms like Coursera and LinkedIn Learning that teach prompt engineering for business professionals.

Looking ahead, the pace of AI development shows no sign of slowing. Anthropic announced Claude 3.7 Sonnet in early 2026 with significantly improved reasoning capabilities. Google released Gemini 2.0 with stronger multimodal abilities, meaning it can handle text, images, audio, and video together. These models are increasingly being embedded into the software that professionals use every day, rather than existing as separate tools. The practical implication is that AI assistance will become a background feature of most knowledge work, much like spell-check became invisible but essential.

For Japanese professionals specifically, there is one more reason to pay attention to AI tools: they can help level the playing field on English. Reading reports in English every day and using AI to check your own writing in English creates a powerful feedback loop. The professionals building this habit today are developing skills that will compound over time.

**Leverage**

*Verb / Noun | /ˈlev.ər.ɪdʒ/ | Origin: from Old French "levier," meaning a tool for lifting — the idea of using a small force to move something large*

**Definition:** As a verb, "leverage" means to use something you already have — a skill, a relationship, a resource — to gain a bigger result. As a noun, it means the power or advantage that comes from using something effectively.

**In context:**
1. "Toyota is trying to leverage its existing dealer network in Southeast Asia to sell more electric vehicles."
2. "The startup had no advertising budget, so it leveraged social media to reach one million users in six months."
3. "You can leverage your Japanese market knowledge to help foreign clients entering this country."

**Why it matters:** "Leverage" appears constantly in business writing, strategy documents, and investor presentations. It signals smart resource use — doing more with what you already have — which is a concept valued in both startup culture and large corporate strategy. Understanding this word helps you read financial reports, strategy memos, and business news with greater confidence.
