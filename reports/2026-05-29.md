## Main Story: Japan's Economy Under Pressure — Trade Tensions, the Yen, and What Comes Next

Japan entered 2026 facing a combination of challenges that would test any major economy. The yen has remained weak against the dollar for an extended period, trade negotiations with the United States have produced more uncertainty than resolution, and domestic consumption has failed to grow at the pace the government hoped. Yet despite these pressures, Japan has also seen real signs of change — in wages, in corporate behavior, and in foreign investment interest. Understanding this full picture is essential for any professional working in or with Japan today.

The exchange rate has been one of the most talked-about topics in Japanese business for the past two years. As of late May 2026, the yen is trading in the range of 150 to 155 yen per dollar. This is a level that was once considered a crisis point, but it has now become almost normal. The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 0.5 percent in early 2025 — its highest level since 2008 — and made a further modest increase to 0.75 percent in early 2026. These moves showed the central bank's confidence that inflation was finally stable. However, because the US Federal Reserve has kept its own rates relatively high, the gap between Japanese and American interest rates remains wide. This gap is the main reason the yen stays weak. For Japanese exporters, a weak yen is not entirely bad news — it makes their products cheaper for overseas buyers. Toyota, Sony, and other manufacturers have reported strong export revenues as a result. But for ordinary Japanese consumers and companies that import raw materials or energy, the weak yen pushes up costs and squeezes household budgets.

Trade policy has added another layer of complexity. The United States, under a policy direction focused on protecting domestic industry, introduced a new round of tariffs in 2025 that affected imports from multiple countries, including Japan. Japanese automakers were particularly exposed. Japan exported approximately 1.3 million vehicles to the United States in 2024, making it the single largest export category in bilateral trade. When Washington signaled tariffs of up to 25 percent on imported cars, it sent a shockwave through the industry. Toyota and Honda both accelerated plans to shift more production to their existing US factories. Nissan, already in a financially difficult position following its struggles in 2024 and 2025, faced even harder decisions about where to build and where to cut. Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) publicly stated that the tariffs were inconsistent with free trade principles and pushed for formal bilateral talks. As of late May 2026, those negotiations were ongoing, with both sides making limited progress on a framework agreement. The outcome will shape not just the auto industry but the entire trade relationship between the two countries.

Wages are one area where Japan has made genuine progress. For the third consecutive year, Japan's major annual wage negotiations — known as "shunto" — produced significant increases. In spring 2026, large companies agreed to average wage hikes of around 5.1 percent, slightly above the 2025 result. The Japanese Trade Union Confederation, known as Rengo, called it a historic trend shift. The government of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who took office in late 2024, made wage growth a central policy priority, arguing that real income gains were the only way to sustainably restart consumer spending. There are signs this is beginning to work. Retail sales data from early 2026 showed modest but consistent growth. Department stores in Tokyo and Osaka reported higher customer spending compared with the same period in 2025. Still, economists caution that inflation — running at around 2.5 percent — continues to eat into real purchasing power for many workers, especially those at smaller companies where wage hikes have been much more modest.

Foreign investment in Japan has become a genuine bright spot. Activist investors and global asset managers have shown increasing interest in Japanese equities, encouraged by corporate governance reforms that pushed companies to improve their return on equity and reduce cross-shareholdings. The Tokyo Stock Exchange's ongoing pressure campaign on listed companies — requiring those trading below book value to explain their improvement plans — has produced results. Dozens of major companies have announced share buybacks, dividend increases, and restructuring plans. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway made headlines in 2023 by increasing stakes in the five major Japanese trading houses, and that interest from global investors has continued to grow. By early 2026, net foreign buying of Japanese equities had reached levels not seen in more than a decade. The Nikkei 225 index, after significant volatility in 2024, stabilized and traded in the range of 37,000 to 40,000 points through the first months of 2026.

For Japanese professionals, this environment creates both pressure and opportunity. The pressure is real: companies are restructuring, supply chains are moving, and the skills that were sufficient five years ago may not be enough today. English ability, data literacy, and cross-border communication skills are increasingly valued. Companies that once operated comfortably within the domestic market are now being pushed — by demographics, by investor pressure, and by policy — to think internationally. The opportunity is equally real. Japan's corporate sector is sitting on enormous cash reserves and is under pressure to deploy that capital more effectively. That means investment in new technology, new markets, and new talent. Professionals who can bridge Japanese organizational culture with global business practices are in high demand.

The bigger picture is one of a country in transition. Japan is not collapsing, but it is not standing still either. The structural challenges — a declining and aging population, sluggish productivity growth, and a heavy reliance on exports — have been known for years. What is different now is that the pressure to address them has intensified. Government policy, corporate governance reform, and external trade pressure are all pushing in the same direction: toward a more dynamic, more competitive, and more internationally engaged Japanese economy. Whether Japan can complete that transition successfully — and how quickly — will be one of the defining economic stories of the next decade.

## Second Story: How Japanese Professionals Are Building Side Income — And Which Skills Pay the Most

The idea of a "side business" — called a "副業" (fukugyō) in Japanese — has moved from taboo to mainstream with remarkable speed. Just five years ago, most large Japanese companies prohibited employees from working outside their primary job. Today, many of those same companies actively encourage it, and the government has supported the shift as a way to improve worker mobility and economic flexibility. If you have thought about earning income outside your main job, this is the right moment to start.

The shift in attitude is backed by policy and data. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare updated its model employment rules in 2018 to allow side work by default, but adoption was slow. The pandemic changed that. Remote work showed companies — and workers — that productivity was not tied to a fixed location or a single employer. By 2025, a survey by Recruit Works Institute found that nearly 30 percent of Japanese workers in their 30s and 40s reported doing some form of paid work outside their main job. That number was less than 10 percent in 2019. Among companies with more than 1,000 employees, more than half now formally permit side businesses, according to METI data from late 2025.

What kind of side work pays well? The answer depends heavily on your existing skills, but some categories stand out clearly. Translation and interpretation is one of the most accessible paths for business professionals with strong English. Japanese-English business translation commands rates of between 3,000 and 8,000 yen per 1,000 characters for professional documents, financial reports, and technical material. Platforms like Gengo and Conyac connect Japanese translators with clients worldwide, though competition is high at the entry level. The real money — and the most interesting work — comes when you specialize. A translator with knowledge of pharmaceutical regulations, legal contracts, or financial reporting can charge significantly more and faces much less competition.

Business consulting for small and medium-sized enterprises is another high-value category. Japan has more than 3 million small businesses, and many of them lack access to the kind of strategic thinking that large corporations take for granted. A professional with corporate experience in supply chain management, digital marketing, or financial planning can offer enormous value to a small business owner who has never had those resources. Platforms like VisasQ and Coconala allow professionals to list their expertise and connect with clients for hourly advisory sessions. Rates on VisasQ for business expertise typically range from 10,000 to 50,000 yen per hour, with experienced professionals in specialized fields charging even more. This is not quick money — building a reputation and a client base takes time — but the income potential is substantial.

Online education and content creation has become a third major category. The global demand for people who can teach Japanese — or teach business skills in Japanese — through online platforms continues to grow. Platforms such as Udemy and Skillshare allow instructors to create courses once and earn ongoing revenue. A well-constructed course on business Japanese, Excel modeling for Japanese finance professionals, or project management in a Japanese corporate context can generate passive income for years after the initial work is complete. Japanese creators on Udemy with well-reviewed courses regularly report monthly earnings of 50,000 to 200,000 yen after their courses become established. The initial investment is time, not money — a smartphone camera, good lighting, and clear audio are sufficient to start.

The most important practical advice is to start narrow. Many professionals make the mistake of trying to offer too many services at once. Instead, identify the single skill from your current job that is most transferable and most valuable to an outside client. If you work in accounting, that might be financial modeling or tax preparation support. If you work in marketing, it might be content strategy or campaign analysis for small brands. Build your first client relationship, deliver excellent work, and ask for a referral. Word of mouth is still the most effective growth engine for freelance professionals in Japan, where trust and personal recommendation carry enormous weight.

Manage the legal and tax side carefully. Side income above 200,000 yen per year must be declared separately in Japan's annual tax filing (確定申告). If you earn more than 1 million yen annually from side work, you may also need to consider consumption tax registration. The good news is that legitimate business expenses — your laptop, internet connection, any software you use for client work, and even a portion of your home office costs — can be deducted. Using accounting software like Freee or MayoBusiness makes this significantly easier. Do not skip this step — clean financial records are a mark of professionalism and protect you if questions arise.

The window of opportunity is open right now. Japanese companies and small businesses are increasingly comfortable hiring freelance talent. Global platforms are making it easier to reach international clients. And the social stigma around side work is fading fast. The professionals who build these skills and income streams now will have more career flexibility, more financial resilience, and more leverage in their primary jobs than those who wait.
