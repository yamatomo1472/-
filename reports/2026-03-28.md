## Main Story: Japan and the World Economy in 2026 — The End of Easy Money and What Comes Next

For most of the past three decades, Japan lived in a world of near-zero interest rates and slow economic growth. That world is ending. The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 0.75 percent in January 2026, its highest level since 1993. This single decision — quiet in tone, enormous in effect — is now rippling through every corner of the Japanese and global economy. Understanding what is happening, and why it matters, is one of the most important things a Japanese professional can do right now.

The story begins with inflation. Japan's consumer price index rose 3.1 percent year-on-year in February 2026, according to Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications. For a country that spent two decades fighting deflation — falling prices — this number is remarkable. Food prices rose sharply, driven partly by a weak yen and partly by global supply chain pressures that began during the COVID-19 pandemic and never fully recovered. Energy costs also pushed higher, as Japan depends heavily on imported oil and gas. The Bank of Japan, led by Governor Kazuo Ueda, watched these numbers carefully for months before deciding that Japan's economy was finally strong enough to handle higher borrowing costs.

The decision to raise rates has real consequences for everyday life. Home loan rates are rising. Banks are now offering fixed 35-year mortgage rates above 2 percent for the first time in years. Families who bought homes using variable-rate loans — a very popular choice in Japan — are now seeing their monthly payments increase. The Japan Housing Finance Agency reported in February 2026 that the share of new borrowers choosing fixed-rate mortgages jumped from 28 percent to 41 percent in just six months, as buyers rushed to lock in rates before they climbed further. This single behavioral shift tells you a great deal about how quickly Japan's financial mood is changing.

For businesses, higher rates mean higher costs. Companies that borrowed cheaply to expand, buy equipment, or invest in real estate now face larger interest bills. Small and medium-sized enterprises — the backbone of Japan's economy, employing roughly 70 percent of all workers — are feeling this most acutely. The Tokyo Shoko Research firm reported in early 2026 that corporate bankruptcies rose 18 percent year-on-year in the final quarter of 2025, driven largely by smaller firms unable to manage both rising costs and rising debt payments. This is a painful but necessary adjustment, economists say. Zombie companies — firms that only survived because borrowing was so cheap — are being forced to restructure or close.

On the global stage, Japan's policy shift is happening against a complicated backdrop. The United States Federal Reserve cut its benchmark rate twice in the second half of 2025, bringing it to 4.25 percent. This narrowed the interest rate gap between the US and Japan, which is one reason the Japanese yen strengthened from around 158 yen per dollar in mid-2025 to approximately 142 yen per dollar by late March 2026. A stronger yen is good news for Japanese consumers and importers — it makes foreign goods cheaper. But it puts pressure on Japan's giant exporters. Toyota, Sony, and Panasonic all issued warnings in their fiscal year earnings guidance that a yen at 140 or stronger would reduce their overseas profits when converted back into yen. Toyota alone estimated that every one-yen strengthening costs the company approximately 45 billion yen in annual operating profit.

The global economy itself is growing, but unevenly. The International Monetary Fund's January 2026 World Economic Outlook projected global growth at 3.2 percent for 2026 — steady but not exciting. The United States remained resilient, growing at around 2.4 percent in 2025. China, by contrast, grew at just 4.6 percent in 2025, below its government target of 5 percent, weighed down by a weak property market and sluggish consumer spending. For Japan, China's slowdown matters enormously. China is Japan's largest trading partner, and Japanese manufacturers — from auto parts makers to semiconductor equipment suppliers — depend on Chinese demand. Companies like Tokyo Electron and Shin-Etsu Chemical reported slower order growth from Chinese customers in their most recent quarterly results.

Southeast Asia is emerging as a partial substitute. Japan's trade with the ASEAN bloc grew 8 percent in 2025, according to Japan's Ministry of Finance data. Japanese manufacturers are building new factories in Vietnam, Thailand, and Indonesia — partly to reduce dependence on China, partly to access growing consumer markets. This trend, often called "China+1 strategy," accelerated sharply after US tariff tensions with Beijing flared again in early 2026. Japanese companies are not leaving China entirely, but they are reducing their exposure. For Japanese professionals in manufacturing, logistics, or supply chain roles, Southeast Asia is becoming a key career frontier.

Geopolitics is also shaping Japan's economic decisions in new ways. Japan increased its defense budget to 2 percent of GDP in 2025, fulfilling a commitment made to NATO partners and the United States. This means Japan is now spending approximately 10 trillion yen per year on defense — a historic shift for a country that maintained a 1 percent cap for decades. The budget increase is creating new business opportunities in aerospace, cybersecurity, and defense technology. Companies like Mitsubishi Heavy Industries and IHI Corporation are expanding their defense divisions rapidly. For young professionals with engineering or technology backgrounds, this sector is now actively hiring.

Back at home, Japan's labor market remains the tightest in decades. The unemployment rate stood at just 2.4 percent in January 2026, and many industries are reporting severe worker shortages. The government has responded by expanding the types of jobs that foreign workers can fill, and the number of foreign residents in Japan exceeded 3.5 million for the first time in 2025. Wages are rising — real wages grew 1.2 percent in 2025 after years of decline — but many workers still feel the pinch of inflation outpacing their salary increases. The 2026 spring wage negotiations, known as shunto, produced an average wage increase of 4.1 percent across major companies, the highest in thirty-three years. This is a significant moment. It suggests Japan may finally be escaping its low-wage, low-inflation trap.

What does all of this mean for you, the Japanese professional reading this on the train? Several things stand out. First, financial decisions you make now — about mortgages, savings, and investments — matter more than they did five years ago. The era of "rates will never change" is over. Second, the industries that are growing — defense, Southeast Asia-linked trade, AI, real estate — are all creating new roles for people with the right skills. Third, the global economy is complex and uncertain, but Japan is no longer sitting still. It is changing, and those who understand the change early will have an advantage.

## Second Story: Tokyo Land Prices Are Hitting Bubble-Era Records — What Japan's Real Estate Boom Means for You

Walk through central Tokyo today and you will see cranes on every horizon. Apartment towers are rising in Minato, Shibuya, and Koto. Luxury condominiums in Azabudai Hills, the massive mixed-use development completed by Mori Building in late 2023, are reselling for prices that would have seemed impossible a decade ago. Japan's real estate market is in the middle of a historic rally — and understanding it could have a real impact on your financial future.

The numbers tell a striking story. Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism released its annual land price survey in March 2026. Commercial land prices in Tokyo rose 10.3 percent year-on-year — the highest jump since 1991, the peak of Japan's famous asset bubble. Residential land in Tokyo's 23 wards rose 7.8 percent. Outside the capital, cities like Osaka, Fukuoka, and Sapporo also posted strong gains. Osaka's commercial land rose 9.1 percent, driven partly by preparations for the 2025 World Expo's lasting infrastructure impact and growing inbound tourism. Fukuoka, often called "Japan's startup city," saw residential prices climb 8.4 percent as younger workers relocated there from Tokyo.

Several forces are driving this boom. First and most importantly: foreign investment. With the yen at historically weak levels for much of 2024 and early 2025, foreign buyers found Japanese property extraordinarily cheap in dollar or euro terms. Blackstone, the American private equity giant, expanded its Japanese real estate portfolio to over 1 trillion yen by mid-2025. Goldman Sachs and GIC, Singapore's sovereign wealth fund, also made major acquisitions in Tokyo commercial property. Even as the yen has strengthened somewhat in early 2026, these investors are not retreating — they are betting on Japan's long-term economic recovery.

Second, domestic buyers are also active, and the reasons are interesting. Japanese households have traditionally kept most of their savings in bank deposits earning almost no interest. The government's expanded NISA tax-free investment program, which launched in its new form in January 2024, pushed millions of Japanese into equities and real estate investment trusts for the first time. At the same time, the stock market rally of 2024 and early 2025 — the Nikkei 225 crossed 40,000 for the first time — created a wealth effect. People who saw their investments grow felt more confident spending on property.

Third, supply is tight. Japan's construction industry faces a severe labor shortage. The number of construction workers in Japan has fallen by more than 25 percent since 1997. Building new homes and apartments takes longer and costs more than it did a decade ago. In Tokyo, the average cost to build a new condominium per square meter rose roughly 35 percent between 2020 and 2025, according to data from the Real Estate Economic Institute. This cost is passed directly to buyers, pushing prices higher. In the most desirable neighborhoods, new condominium prices per square meter now routinely exceed 2 million yen — prices that once only existed in Ginza or the most exclusive parts of Minato.

So what does this mean if you are a Japanese professional thinking about property? First, if you already own property in a major city, your asset has likely grown significantly in value. Some owners who bought ten or fifteen years ago have seen their property value double. Second, if you are thinking about buying, the decision is now more complex than it was three years ago. Higher mortgage rates and higher prices together mean monthly payments on a new purchase can be surprisingly large. A 70-square-meter apartment in a central Tokyo ward that sold for 70 million yen in 2022 might sell for 95 million yen today. At a 35-year mortgage with a 2.1 percent fixed rate, monthly payments would exceed 310,000 yen. That is a significant commitment.

Regional cities offer a genuinely different calculus. In Fukuoka, a comparable apartment might cost 45 to 55 million yen. In Sendai or Hiroshima, prices are lower still. As remote work becomes more normalized in Japan — slowly, but it is happening — some professionals are choosing to live in regional cities and commute to Tokyo once or twice a week for important meetings. This lifestyle arbitrage is a real financial strategy. You spend less on housing, face less daily commuting stress, and still maintain your Tokyo career. Companies like Recruit Holdings and Mercari have made flexible location work permanent policy, and others are following.

The rental market is also shifting. Rental prices in central Tokyo rose 6.2 percent in 2025, according to data from Homes.co.jp. This is partly because some potential buyers are waiting, watching rates — and staying renters longer. It is also because inbound tourism created demand for short-term rental platforms, which removed some units from the long-term rental market. If you own property, the rental yield calculation has become more attractive than it was in the zero-rate era. A well-located apartment in Tokyo can now generate a gross rental yield of 3.5 to 4.5 percent annually — still modest by global standards, but far better than a bank deposit.

One practical takeaway stands out above all others: location still matters more than anything else. Properties near major train lines, new development hubs like Toranomon or Takanawa Gateway, and universities or hospitals are holding value better than suburban or rural properties. The gap between winning and losing locations in Japan is growing. Japan's population is declining — it fell below 124 million in 2025 — and that means demand for property in shrinking towns is falling even as it rises in cities. If you are thinking about property as an investment, focus on where people want to live, not just where prices seem cheap.
