## Main Story: Japan Between Two Giants — How the U.S.-China Trade War Is Reshaping Japan's Economy in 2026

Japan has always had a complicated relationship with both the United States and China. The United States is Japan's most important security partner. China is Japan's largest trading partner. For most of the past two decades, Japan managed to keep both relationships working at the same time. In 2026, that balancing act is becoming much harder.

The background is important. In early 2025, the United States under President Donald Trump launched a new round of tariffs on imports from dozens of countries. Japan was not spared. Washington placed a 24 percent tariff on Japanese goods in April 2025, though this was later paused for 90 days as negotiations began. By late 2025 and into early 2026, those negotiations were still ongoing. Japan's trade minister Yoji Muto made several trips to Washington, and the two governments remained in active talks. Japan wanted a deal that protected its key export industries — especially cars, steel, and electronics.

The numbers make clear why Japan is worried. Japan exported roughly 1.5 trillion yen worth of cars to the United States in 2024. The auto industry alone supports millions of jobs across Japan, including suppliers, parts makers, and logistics companies. A 25 percent tariff on Japanese cars, which Trump also threatened in early 2025, would make Japanese vehicles significantly more expensive in the American market. Toyota, Honda, and Nissan all publicly warned about the damage such tariffs would cause to their business models and to their U.S. operations, where they also employ hundreds of thousands of American workers.

At the same time, China's economy is sending mixed signals. China's GDP growth slowed to around 4.6 percent in 2025, below its official target of five percent. Demand for Japanese manufactured goods inside China weakened as Chinese consumers became more cautious with their spending. Chinese companies also grew stronger as competitors in sectors like electric vehicles, where brands like BYD now compete directly with Japanese automakers both in China and in third markets across Southeast Asia and Latin America.

Japan's response has been to try to diversify. The government under Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who took office in October 2024 after winning the Liberal Democratic Party leadership race, has pushed hard for deeper economic ties with India and with Southeast Asian nations. Japan's direct investment into India reached record levels in fiscal year 2025, with major deals in semiconductors, clean energy, and infrastructure. The Japan-India trade corridor is now one of the fastest-growing economic partnerships in Asia.

At home, the Japanese government launched a major industrial strategy centered on semiconductors and AI. The RAPIDUS project, Japan's attempt to build a domestic advanced chip manufacturing industry with support from IBM and with government funding exceeding 3.3 trillion yen, is now in its critical construction phase in Hokkaido. The first test chips are expected in 2027. METI, Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, has framed this project as essential for Japan's technological independence. The idea is simple: Japan cannot afford to depend entirely on Taiwan or South Korea for the most advanced chips, especially given the security risks in the Taiwan Strait.

The Bank of Japan has been navigating all of this while managing a historic shift in monetary policy. After decades of near-zero interest rates, the BOJ under Governor Kazuo Ueda raised rates to 0.5 percent in January 2025 and again signaled possible further increases through 2026. This is a big change. Higher rates mean the yen is gradually strengthening, which is good news for Japanese consumers buying imported goods but puts pressure on exporters. A stronger yen means the overseas earnings that companies like Toyota bring home are worth less in yen terms.

Inflation remains a concern for ordinary Japanese people. Core consumer prices rose by around 2.5 percent year-on-year in early 2026, above the BOJ's 2 percent target. Workers have seen wage increases — the 2025 spring labor negotiations, the so-called shunto, produced the largest average wage increases in 33 years, with major companies agreeing to raises above five percent. But many workers at smaller companies did not see the same gains, and the cost of food, energy, and housing has eaten into real purchasing power for many households.

For Japanese business professionals, this environment creates both pressure and opportunity. Companies are accelerating digital transformation to cut costs and stay competitive. AI tools are now standard in many large Japanese firms, from customer service to financial analysis. Fujitsu, NTT Data, and NEC have all launched major AI service divisions targeting Japanese corporate clients. The demand for employees who understand both business operations and digital tools is growing fast. English skills are increasingly a real advantage, because so much of the cutting-edge information in technology and finance comes first in English.

The trade situation is also changing where Japanese companies want to invest. The push toward reshoring — bringing supply chains back closer to Japan or into trusted partner countries — is creating new roles in procurement, logistics, and supply chain management. Companies are hiring people who understand geopolitical risk, not just traditional business skills. Knowing how to read and interpret news about tariffs, diplomatic relationships, and technology policy is becoming a practical professional skill, not just background knowledge.

What should you watch in the next six months? First, the outcome of the Japan-U.S. trade talks is critical. Any deal that gives Japan preferential tariff treatment will be a major positive for Japanese manufacturers and for market confidence. Second, watch the yen. If the BOJ raises rates again in July or September 2026 as some analysts expect, the yen could strengthen further, changing the competitive picture for exporters. Third, watch China. Any recovery in Chinese consumer demand would help Japanese companies that still rely heavily on that market, including luxury goods makers, cosmetics brands, and machinery exporters. The situation is uncertain, but uncertainty itself is important information for anyone making career or business decisions right now.

## Second Story: How Japanese Professionals Are Building Side Businesses in the Age of AI Tools and Remote Work

Something is changing in the way Japanese professionals think about their careers. For decades, the dominant model was simple: join a large company, stay loyal, and build your career inside that one organization. That model has not disappeared, but it is under real pressure. More Japanese workers — especially those in their late twenties and thirties — are building side businesses alongside their main jobs. And AI tools are making that easier than ever before.

The legal environment has shifted. In 2022, Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare updated its model employment guidelines to make side jobs the default option, meaning companies should allow them unless there is a specific business reason not to. By 2025, surveys by Recruit Holdings showed that over 40 percent of Japanese office workers had either started a side business or were actively considering one. That number is higher among people who work in tech, marketing, and consulting roles, where skills are directly transferable to freelance work.

What kinds of side businesses are actually working? The most successful ones tend to use skills that people already have from their main jobs. A financial analyst at a large bank, for example, might offer financial modeling consulting to small businesses on weekends. A marketing manager at a consumer goods company might help startups build their brand strategy for a monthly retainer. An engineer with experience in manufacturing might advise smaller suppliers on process improvement. The key insight is that your professional knowledge has real market value outside your employer.

AI tools have made the practical work of running a side business much more manageable. Tools like Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini can help with writing proposals, translating documents, preparing presentations, and drafting emails in English. For Japanese freelancers who want to reach international clients, AI-assisted English writing has removed a major barrier. A designer who previously could only take Japanese-language clients can now communicate professionally with clients in Singapore, Australia, or Europe. The effective cost of starting a knowledge-based side business has fallen dramatically.

Platforms have also made it easier to find clients. Lancers and Crowdworks remain the dominant Japanese freelance platforms, with Lancers reporting over 1.3 million registered users as of early 2026. But many experienced professionals are finding higher-paying work through LinkedIn, Toptal, and direct referrals from professional networks. The international freelance market pays significantly more than the domestic Japanese market for many skills. A Japanese engineer with experience in Toyota's production system, for example, can command very high consulting rates from foreign manufacturers who want to improve their operations.

The tax and legal side is manageable but requires attention. Freelance income above 200,000 yen per year in Japan must be declared separately in your annual tax filing. If your side income grows significantly, registering as a sole proprietor (kojin jigyo) can reduce your tax burden by allowing you to deduct business expenses. Many professionals in Japan avoid dealing with this, but the rules are actually straightforward once you understand them. There are now several accounting apps designed specifically for Japanese freelancers, including freee and MoneyForward, that make tax preparation much simpler.

The most important thing for anyone thinking about a side business is to start with one specific service for one specific type of client, and price it based on value rather than time. Many first-time freelancers underprice their work because they compare themselves to junior staff rates rather than to the actual value of the outcome they deliver. A consultant who helps a small company increase its sales by two million yen per year is delivering clear, measurable value. That is worth far more than charging by the hour at a modest rate.

The risks are real but manageable. The biggest risk is time. A side business that takes 10 hours per week is sustainable alongside a full-time job for many people, but 30 hours per week will cause burnout quickly. The second risk is conflict of interest — taking on clients who compete with your main employer can create serious legal and professional problems. Be very clear about what your employment contract says about side work before you begin.

For Japanese professionals in 2026, building a side business is not just about extra income. It is also about building skills, expanding your network, and creating career options. If your main employer faces layoffs, mergers, or restructuring — all of which are happening across multiple Japanese industries right now — having an established freelance client base is genuine financial security. The best time to start building that base is before you need it.

**Diversify**

*Verb | /daɪˈvɜːrsɪfaɪ/ | Origin: from Latin "diversus" (turned in different directions), entering English business usage in the 20th century*

**Definition:** To expand into new areas, products, markets, or investments so that you are not dependent on just one source. Companies and individuals diversify to reduce risk and find new sources of growth.

**In context:**
1. "Toyota announced plans to diversify its revenue by expanding into energy storage and home robotics alongside its core automotive business."
2. "After years of relying almost entirely on domestic clients, the consultant decided to diversify her client base by targeting companies in Singapore and Australia."
3. "Our team leader suggested we diversify our supplier network to avoid the problems we had last year when our main factory in China was disrupted."

**Why it matters:** "Diversify" and its related noun "diversification" appear constantly in business strategy discussions, investor reports, and economic policy documents. Any time a company, country, or individual is trying to reduce risk by spreading across different areas, this word will appear.
