## Main Story: Japan Navigates Trade Turbulence as the Yen Stays Weak and Global Pressure Builds

The year 2026 is testing Japan's economy in ways not seen in decades. The Japanese yen has spent much of the past two years hovering in a range that few economists predicted would last this long. The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 0.75 percent in early 2026, a historic move by Japanese standards, yet the yen has remained weak against the US dollar, trading in the 150–155 range for much of April and May. For a country that imports most of its energy and a large share of its food, a weak yen is not just a financial headline — it is a daily reality that raises the cost of living for ordinary people and squeezes the margins of small businesses.

The Bank of Japan's cautious approach to rate hikes reflects a genuine dilemma. Governor Kazuo Ueda has said repeatedly that the bank wants to see sustained wage growth before it commits to a faster tightening path. The spring wage negotiations — known as shunto — delivered positive results again in 2026, with major companies including Toyota, Sony, and Nippon Steel agreeing to wage increases averaging around 5 percent. That is strong by historical standards. However, smaller companies, which employ the majority of Japan's workforce, have struggled to match those numbers. The gap between large and small firms is one of the most important stories inside the Japanese economy right now, and it is one that rarely gets the attention it deserves in international media.

Meanwhile, US trade policy is adding another layer of complexity. The tariff measures introduced by the Trump administration in 2025 sent shockwaves through global supply chains, and Japan was not spared. Japanese automakers — including Toyota, Honda, and Nissan — export millions of vehicles to the United States each year. When the US imposed broad tariffs on imported cars, the impact on Japanese manufacturers was immediate and significant. Toyota responded by announcing an expansion of its US-based manufacturing capacity, pledging to invest an additional 13 billion dollars in American plants over the next three years. Honda made similar moves. This is the playbook for large Japanese firms: when trade walls go up, build inside the wall.

But this strategy is not available to every company. Mid-sized Japanese manufacturers and parts suppliers often lack the capital to shift production overseas quickly. Many of them have been lobbying the Japanese government to negotiate a bilateral trade deal with the United States that would reduce tariff exposure. As of May 2026, those negotiations are ongoing. Japan's chief trade negotiator, Ryosei Akazawa, has held multiple rounds of talks with US Treasury officials, and both sides have described the discussions as constructive. However, no deal has been signed, and Japanese exporters remain in a state of uncertainty.

The situation in China adds even more pressure. Japan and China are each other's largest trading partners, but the relationship has grown more complicated over the past three years. China's economy has slowed considerably from its earlier growth rates. The International Monetary Fund revised its 2026 growth forecast for China down to 4.2 percent in its April World Economic Outlook — a far cry from the double-digit growth rates that defined the early 2000s. Chinese consumer demand for Japanese goods, from cars to cosmetics to electronics, has softened. At the same time, Chinese manufacturers have become stronger competitors in sectors like electric vehicles, putting direct pressure on Japanese automakers in third markets across Southeast Asia and Europe.

Japan's government has responded with a clear strategic pivot toward Southeast Asia and India. Prime Minister Fumio Kishida's successor has continued and expanded the Free and Open Indo-Pacific framework as a diplomatic and economic strategy. Japanese companies are investing heavily in Vietnam, Indonesia, India, and the Philippines. According to data from Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, Japanese foreign direct investment into ASEAN countries rose by approximately 18 percent in fiscal year 2025. India is now a particular focus. Japanese companies see India's large and growing middle class, its relatively young population, and its government's strong interest in attracting foreign capital as a powerful combination. Companies like Suzuki, which has dominated India's small car market for decades, are doubling down on local production and electric vehicle development there.

For Japanese professionals working in trade, logistics, or manufacturing, these shifts have direct career implications. Companies are looking for people who understand Southeast Asian markets, who can manage cross-cultural teams, and who speak English well enough to negotiate with overseas partners. The demand for internationally minded Japanese professionals — people who can bridge the gap between Japanese corporate culture and global business norms — is rising. This is one reason why English skills are increasingly seen not as a bonus but as a core professional requirement.

Back at home, Japan's domestic economy shows some encouraging signs alongside the challenges. Tourism has been a bright spot. The weak yen has made Japan an extremely attractive destination for foreign visitors. The Japan Tourism Agency reported that Japan received over 40 million international visitors in 2025, surpassing previous records and generating substantial revenue for the hospitality, retail, and transport sectors. However, this tourism boom has also created visible tension in popular destinations like Kyoto, Osaka, and Hokkaido, where local residents have raised concerns about crowding and the rising cost of living partly linked to tourist demand.

The labor market remains tight almost everywhere. Japan's unemployment rate has stayed below 2.5 percent, and companies across industries are competing hard to attract and retain workers. This environment gives workers more bargaining power than they have had in a long time. It also creates pressure on companies to automate and use technology more effectively — which is accelerating Japan's interest in artificial intelligence tools and digital transformation initiatives.

The overall picture is one of an economy at a genuine turning point. Japan is no longer stuck in the deflationary trap that defined the 1990s and 2000s. Prices are rising, wages are rising, and companies are being pushed to change. But this transition is uneven and sometimes painful. For a business professional watching these developments, the message is clear: the old assumptions about stable prices, slow change, and lifetime employment with one company are giving way to something more dynamic, more uncertain, and in many ways more interesting.

Understanding these macro trends — the yen's trajectory, the US-Japan trade relationship, China's slowdown, and Southeast Asia's rise — is not just background knowledge. It shapes which industries will grow, which companies will thrive, and which skills will be most valuable in the years ahead. Staying informed is itself a competitive advantage.

## Second Story: America's Startup Machine Keeps Running — What Japan Can Learn from the 2025–2026 VC Boom

American venture capital had a difficult couple of years after the peak of 2021, when funding levels hit record highs and then fell sharply in 2022 and 2023. But the recovery that began in late 2024 has continued strongly into 2026, and the startup ecosystem looks different from before — more focused, more disciplined, and more powered by artificial intelligence than anyone predicted even five years ago.

Total US venture capital investment in 2025 reached approximately 285 billion dollars, according to data tracked by PitchBook and the National Venture Capital Association. That is not quite back to the frenzied levels of 2021, but it is a healthy, sustainable number that reflects genuine business activity rather than speculative excess. The biggest story inside those numbers is how concentrated the investment has become. A large share of the total — some analysts estimate as much as 40 percent — went into AI-related companies. This includes infrastructure companies building the hardware and cloud services that power AI, platform companies building the tools and APIs that developers use, and application companies putting AI to work in specific industries like healthcare, legal services, education, and logistics.

The companies attracting the most attention in early 2026 include names that were barely known two years ago. Perplexity AI, the search startup backed by Jeff Bezos and others, raised significant new funding and crossed a valuation of 14 billion dollars. Anysphere, the company behind the Cursor code editor, reportedly reached profitability faster than almost any developer tools company in history, driven by the explosive demand from software engineers who use AI assistance to write code. Glean, an enterprise search and knowledge management company, expanded its customer base rapidly as large corporations sought tools to help employees find and use information more efficiently.

What makes this wave of startup activity different from the boom of 2020 and 2021 is the focus on revenue. Investors who were burned by companies that grew fast but never made money are now insisting on a clearer path to profitability. The phrase that appears most often in venture capital discussions right now is "efficient growth" — the idea that a startup should grow its revenue faster than it grows its costs. This sounds obvious, but it represents a real shift in how Silicon Valley and its investors think about success.

For Japanese professionals, this trend is directly relevant. Many large Japanese corporations have set up venture investment arms or innovation labs to watch and participate in the US startup scene. SoftBank remains the most prominent Japanese player, with its Vision Fund continuing to make bets on late-stage technology companies around the world. But other Japanese firms — including NTT, Sony, Recruit Holdings, and a growing number of mid-sized manufacturers — are also sending teams to Silicon Valley and building relationships with American startups. The goal is not just financial return. It is learning how to move faster, experiment more, and build new business models before competitors do.

The geographic spread of US startup activity is also worth noting. While San Francisco and the Bay Area remain the center of gravity for AI and deep tech, other cities have grown significantly. New York has become a powerhouse for fintech, media, and enterprise software. Austin has attracted a large number of companies that want lower costs and a different lifestyle. Miami made a serious bid to become a tech hub during the remote work years and has retained more of that energy than skeptics expected. This geographic diversity makes the US startup ecosystem more resilient and also creates more opportunities for foreign companies to build partnerships in different parts of the country.

One specific area that Japanese companies are watching closely is B2B software for manufacturing and supply chain management. American startups like Tulip Interfaces, Fictiv, and Bright Machines are bringing software and robotics tools to factory floors in ways that could transform how physical goods are made and tracked. Japanese manufacturers, who are world leaders in production quality and process discipline, are both potential customers for these tools and potential partners or competitors. Several Japanese corporations have already made direct investments in US manufacturing tech startups, and METI has encouraged this kind of cross-border technology transfer as part of Japan's industrial policy.

The key insight for any Japanese professional following this space is that the US startup ecosystem is not just a financial story. It is a signal about where the economy is going. The companies getting funded today are solving problems that large enterprises — including major Japanese corporations — will face tomorrow. Watching which sectors attract capital, which business models are winning, and which technologies are moving from experiment to mainstream product is one of the most efficient ways to stay ahead of change in your own industry.

## 📖 Word of the Day

**Headwind**

*Noun | /ˈhɛd.wɪnd/ | Origin: from the literal meaning of a wind blowing directly against the direction of travel, used in aviation and sailing before entering business and economic language in the late 20th century*

**Definition:** A headwind is a condition or factor that makes progress harder or slows growth. In business and economics, it refers to challenges like rising costs, weak demand, or unfavorable policy that work against a company or economy.

**In context:**
1. "Japanese exporters face significant headwinds from the strong dollar and ongoing US tariff policy."
2. "The CFO warned investors that rising raw material costs would be a headwind to profit margins for the next two quarters."
3. "We should prepare our clients for headwinds in the second half of the year, as interest rates remain elevated."

**Why it matters:** This word appears constantly in earnings calls, economic reports, and financial journalism. Understanding it — and its opposite, "tailwind" — allows you to read analyst reports and business news much more efficiently. Mastering these metaphors is a key step to reading financial English with confidence.
