## Main Story: Japan's Economy Faces a Defining Moment as Trade Tensions and Inflation Reshape the Business Landscape

Japan entered 2026 facing a combination of challenges that economists have rarely seen at the same time. The yen is trading near historically weak levels against the dollar. Inflation remains above the Bank of Japan's 2% target but is driven by import costs rather than strong domestic demand. And the United States, Japan's most important ally and trading partner, has been pushing hard on tariff policy under the second Trump administration. As the first quarter of 2026 closes, understanding these forces is essential for any Japanese professional who works in trade, manufacturing, finance, or business strategy.

The yen weakened significantly through 2025 and has continued to sit in a range that makes imports expensive for Japanese consumers and companies. In early 2026, the yen was trading at roughly 150 to 155 yen per dollar, levels that the Bank of Japan once considered an emergency but now treats as a new normal. This weak yen hurts Japanese households directly. Energy, food, and raw materials all become more expensive when the yen is weak, because Japan imports nearly all of its oil and a large share of its food. Consumer prices in Japan rose by around 3% year-on-year in early 2026, according to data from Japan's Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications. That does not sound dramatic compared to inflation peaks in the United States or Europe, but for Japan, which spent three decades fighting deflation, sustained inflation above 2% is a serious structural shift.

The Bank of Japan, led by Governor Kazuo Ueda, raised interest rates carefully in 2024 and 2025, moving away from the ultra-loose monetary policy that defined Japan for years. By early 2026, the BOJ's policy rate had risen to around 0.75%, a level that was unthinkable five years ago. However, the BOJ has been cautious about moving faster because the Japanese economy is not growing strongly enough to justify aggressive rate hikes. Real wage growth has been inconsistent. Many workers received nominal pay raises in the 2025 spring wage negotiations, known as Shunto, but inflation ate into those gains. The 2026 Shunto negotiations are now concluding, and early results suggest average wage increases of around 5%, which major unions at large companies like Toyota, Sony, and Panasonic agreed to. Whether smaller companies can match those increases is a separate and important question.

Japan's trade relationship with the United States has become more complicated under the Trump administration's second term. The US has pushed Japan to reduce its trade surplus with America, which stood at around $70 billion in goods in 2025, according to US Census Bureau data. Washington has threatened tariffs on Japanese automobiles and electronics, which are among Japan's most important export categories. Japan's automobile industry, led by Toyota, Honda, Nissan, and Mazda, is particularly vulnerable. The US market accounts for a large share of revenue for all of these companies. Toyota, which remains the world's largest carmaker by volume, has responded by expanding its US manufacturing footprint, investing in new facilities in states like Kentucky and Texas. However, not all Japanese automakers have the financial scale to rapidly shift production.

The semiconductor sector offers a more positive story. Japan has worked hard to rebuild its chip manufacturing capacity, partly with support from the Japanese government through the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry. The establishment of TSMC's Kumamoto facility, which began production in 2024, marked a historic shift. A second TSMC Kumamoto plant is now under construction. Alongside TSMC, Rapidus, the Japanese government-backed chipmaker, is building advanced semiconductor manufacturing capability in Hokkaido, targeting 2nm chip production. These investments show that Japan is serious about rebuilding its technological industrial base after decades of decline in semiconductor manufacturing. For professionals in tech, engineering, and business development, this creates real opportunities.

Japan's relationship with China also shapes the economic picture in complex ways. China remains Japan's largest trading partner by total volume, but the relationship carries risks. Japan has followed US-led restrictions on exporting certain advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment to China. Companies like Tokyo Electron and Shin-Etsu Chemical have faced difficult choices about how to manage their China exposure. Meanwhile, Japanese companies are diversifying their supply chains toward Southeast Asia, India, and other markets. This "China plus one" strategy has become standard thinking in Japanese corporate boardrooms.

Domestically, Japan is trying to address a labor shortage that has become one of its most serious structural problems. Japan's working-age population is shrinking every year. The government has expanded its acceptance of foreign workers through revised immigration rules, and more workers from Vietnam, the Philippines, Indonesia, and other Asian countries are now part of Japan's workforce. This is a real cultural and social shift for a country that has historically been reluctant to open its borders to large-scale immigration. Companies across manufacturing, retail, hospitality, and care services are adapting to a more international workforce.

The Tokyo Stock Exchange has also been in the spotlight. The TSE pushed Japanese companies to improve their price-to-book ratios and shareholder returns, a reform campaign that began gaining momentum in 2023 and has continued. More companies are buying back shares, raising dividends, and unwinding cross-shareholdings. Foreign investors have taken notice. Net foreign investment into Japanese equities was strong in 2024 and continued in 2025. The Nikkei 225 index touched record highs in 2024 and has experienced some volatility in early 2026 due to yen movements and global uncertainty, but remains at historically elevated levels.

For Japanese professionals in business, the picture is genuinely mixed but dynamic. The weak yen creates pain for consumers but helps exporters. The labor shortage creates pressure but also creates opportunity for people with rare skills. The government's investment in semiconductors, green energy, and digital transformation is creating new industries. The trade tensions with the US require careful navigation but also push Japanese companies to become more competitive and more global. Understanding these dynamics — not just at the surface level but in their detail — is what separates professionals who can contribute strategic thinking from those who simply execute tasks. The macro environment in Japan today is one of the most interesting and consequential in a generation.

The American venture capital world ended 2025 in a very different shape than it started. After two years of correction following the 2021 peak, deal volumes and valuations are recovering. Artificial intelligence remains the defining theme. OpenAI raised additional capital at a valuation exceeding $150 billion. Anthropic, the AI safety company backed by Amazon and Google, raised multiple large rounds. xAI, Elon Musk's AI company, secured billions in funding. Investors who are not focused on AI are looking very carefully at sectors where AI will cause disruption and create new needs. Cybersecurity is one clear example. As AI tools become more powerful, the attack surface for digital systems grows larger. Startups building AI-native cybersecurity tools raised significant capital in 2025. Healthcare technology is another area. AI-powered diagnostics, drug discovery platforms, and clinical workflow automation attracted serious investor attention. Companies like Recursion Pharmaceuticals and Tempus AI showed that the intersection of AI and biology is commercially real, not just theoretical.

Climate technology, often called cleantech, had a more complex year. The political environment under the Trump administration reduced federal support for certain green energy programs. However, private capital continued to flow into areas where the economics are already compelling without subsidies. Battery storage, grid modernization, and industrial decarbonization all attracted investment. Geographically, San Francisco and the broader Bay Area reasserted its dominance as the center of AI startup activity. The density of AI talent in San Francisco — drawn partly by Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and dozens of smaller firms — has created an ecosystem effect that other cities are struggling to match. New York remains strong in fintech, media, and enterprise software. Austin continues to grow as a tech hub, drawing companies and talent from California. The venture capital fund landscape itself changed in important ways. Mega-funds — those with more than $1 billion under management — continued to dominate fundraising, but many mid-sized and smaller specialized funds also raised capital successfully. Investors who focused narrowly on specific sectors, such as defense technology, agricultural technology, or legal tech, found willing limited partners who wanted focused exposure. Sequoia Capital, Andreessen Horowitz, and Benchmark remained the most recognized names, but newer firms like Conviction Capital and several AI-focused micro-funds built strong reputations based on early bets that paid off.

For Japanese professionals, the most actionable insight from the US startup world is about the types of problems that attract the most funding right now. Investors want to back companies that reduce the cost of knowledge work through automation, that solve legacy infrastructure problems in industries like healthcare and manufacturing, and that build security and trust in an increasingly AI-mediated world. These are problems that Japanese companies face too. Japanese enterprises are famously slow to adopt new technology, which creates a real market for smart B2B startups — both American ones expanding into Japan and Japanese ones solving problems for local corporate clients. Watching where US venture capital flows gives you a roughly two-to-three year preview of what business problems will become standard priorities for Japanese companies.

The exit environment also improved in late 2025 and early 2026. After a long drought in IPOs, the US public markets reopened somewhat for tech companies. A handful of AI and software companies listed successfully, giving investors returns and refreshing the venture ecosystem's appetite for new bets. M&A activity also picked up, with large technology companies acquiring startups in AI, security, and data infrastructure. This creates a healthier cycle: exits return money to limited partners, who reinvest in new funds, which back new startups. The startup ecosystem runs on this cycle, and the fact that it is turning again is a positive signal.
