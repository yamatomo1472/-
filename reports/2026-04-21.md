## Main Story: Japan's Economy Under Pressure — The Yen, Trade Policy, and What Comes Next

Japan entered 2026 facing a difficult combination of forces. The yen remains weak, trading near 150 to the US dollar for much of early 2026. Trade relations between Japan and the United States have become more complicated. And the Bank of Japan is still trying to find the right balance between controlling inflation and keeping growth alive. For Japanese professionals, understanding these forces is not just background knowledge — it is directly relevant to business decisions, salary negotiations, and career strategy.

The yen's weakness has been the dominant story in Japan's economy for several years now. After the Bank of Japan raised interest rates modestly in 2024 and again in early 2025, many analysts expected the yen to recover. Instead, the currency stayed weak. The reason is simple: US interest rates remain higher than Japan's, which means investors prefer to hold dollars. When global investors move money into dollar assets, they sell yen, and the currency falls. As of early 2026, the yen trades between 148 and 153 per dollar, depending on the week. This is far weaker than the 110 level seen in 2021.

A weak yen has two sides. For Japanese exporters like Toyota, Sony, and Fanuc, it is good news. When they earn money overseas and bring it back to Japan, it converts into more yen. Toyota reported record profits in its fiscal year 2025 results, driven partly by this currency effect. But for ordinary Japanese people and companies that import goods, a weak yen means higher prices. Energy, food, and raw materials all cost more. The Ministry of Finance reported that Japan's import bill stayed elevated in late 2025, putting pressure on household budgets and smaller businesses that rely on imported inputs.

Trade policy has added another layer of uncertainty. The United States under the current administration has pushed hard on trade deficits. Japan runs a significant trade surplus with the US, which has made it a target for tariff pressure. In early 2026, Washington and Tokyo were in active negotiations over a new bilateral trade framework. The US wanted Japan to buy more American agricultural products and open its auto market further. Japan, for its part, pushed back on tariffs that could hurt its car exports, which remain critical to companies like Toyota, Honda, and Subaru. Reuters reported in March 2026 that both sides were aiming for a deal before summer, but the details remained contested.

The stakes for Japan's auto industry are enormous. Cars and auto parts account for roughly 28 percent of Japan's total exports to the United States. Any new tariff on Japanese vehicles could cost the industry billions of dollars per year. Toyota alone employs tens of thousands of people in the US through its manufacturing plants in Kentucky, Texas, and Indiana. This gives Japan some leverage in negotiations, since US politicians from those states do not want Toyota to scale back investment. Still, the uncertainty is real, and companies are making contingency plans.

Beyond autos, the trade discussions touch on semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, and digital services. Japan has positioned itself as a key partner in the US strategy to reduce dependence on Chinese technology. The new TSMC plant in Kumamoto, which began production in 2024, is a symbol of this alignment. A second TSMC facility in Kumamoto is under construction, expected to open by 2027. The Japanese government has committed over two trillion yen in subsidies to support semiconductor manufacturing on home soil. Nikkei Asia reported in February 2026 that Rapidus, the Japanese chip startup backed by the government and companies like Toyota and Sony, is targeting mass production of cutting-edge two-nanometer chips by 2027. These projects represent Japan's most serious attempt in decades to build a technology industry that can compete globally.

The Bank of Japan's policy path also deserves attention. Governor Kazuo Ueda has moved carefully since taking office in 2023. The BOJ ended its negative interest rate policy in March 2024 and has raised rates twice since then, bringing the policy rate to 0.75 percent as of early 2026. This is still low by global standards, but it is a significant change for Japan, which lived with near-zero or negative rates for over a decade. The BOJ has signaled that it will continue raising rates gradually if inflation stays above its two percent target. Core inflation in Japan ran at around 2.5 percent in the first quarter of 2026, according to BOJ data. This gives the central bank room to move, but also creates challenges for companies with heavy debt loads and for the government, which carries one of the highest debt-to-GDP ratios in the world.

For Japanese professionals, these macro trends translate into concrete workplace realities. Companies in export-heavy industries are doing well, but many smaller firms are struggling with higher costs. Wage growth has been a positive story — Rengo, Japan's largest trade union federation, secured an average wage increase of over five percent in the 2026 spring wage negotiations, the second consecutive year of strong gains. This is good for workers, but it also means companies are under more cost pressure. Some firms are investing in automation and AI tools to manage labor costs, which is reshaping what skills are valued in the job market.

What does all of this mean practically? First, if you work for an exporter or a company connected to the semiconductor supply chain, you are in a relatively strong position. Demand for people who understand both business and technology is rising. Second, if your company imports materials or relies on foreign suppliers, cost management skills are increasingly valuable. Understanding currency risk and supply chain finance will set you apart. Third, the trade negotiations between Japan and the US will continue to create news and uncertainty through mid-2026 at least. Staying informed about the outcome of these talks is not just intellectually interesting — it has direct implications for which industries will grow and which will face headwinds over the next two to three years.

Japan is not facing a crisis. But it is facing a genuine test. The choices made in trade policy, technology investment, and monetary policy in 2026 will shape the country's economic position for the rest of the decade. ## Second Story: America's Startup Ecosystem Is Shifting — What Japanese Professionals Need to Know

The American startup world looked very different in early 2026 compared to two years ago. After a painful correction in 2022 and 2023, venture capital funding recovered strongly in 2024 and accelerated again in 2025. But the money is not flowing evenly. Artificial intelligence dominates the investment landscape in a way that no single technology has since the smartphone era. For Japanese professionals watching from across the Pacific, or thinking about building their own ventures, understanding how this ecosystem works right now is genuinely useful.

Total US venture capital investment reached approximately 285 billion dollars in 2025, according to data from PitchBook and the National Venture Capital Association. That was up significantly from 2023's low of around 170 billion dollars. The recovery was led almost entirely by AI. OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and dozens of smaller AI companies attracted enormous rounds. OpenAI alone raised over 40 billion dollars in late 2024 and early 2025, giving it a valuation above 300 billion dollars — making it one of the most valuable private companies ever. Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI assistant, raised multiple large rounds backed by Google and Amazon. The concentration of capital into AI has been striking. Some analysts estimate that AI-related startups captured over 40 percent of all US venture funding in 2025.

Beyond the headline AI giants, the more interesting story is what is happening at the application layer. Hundreds of startups are building products that use large language models — the technology behind ChatGPT and Claude — to solve specific business problems. Legal tech startups are automating contract review. Healthcare companies are building AI tools for diagnosis support and patient communication. Finance startups are using AI to detect fraud, generate reports, and advise retail investors. These application-layer startups are often smaller, but they are growing fast and solving real problems. Many of them are generating revenue within their first year, which was unusual in the previous startup era when companies often prioritized growth over profit for years.

This shift toward faster revenue generation reflects a broader change in investor expectations. The era of "growth at all costs" ended sharply in 2022 when interest rates rose and easy money disappeared. Today's venture investors want to see a clear path to profitability. They ask harder questions about unit economics — meaning, does the company make money on each customer it serves? This discipline has made the surviving startup companies stronger. It has also made the US startup ecosystem more selective. Fewer companies are getting funded, but those that do get funded tend to be more serious and better run.

For Japanese professionals, there are two angles worth considering here. The first is the career angle. The second angle is the entrepreneurial one. The tools available to first-time founders in 2026 are dramatically better than they were five years ago. AI coding assistants like Claude Code and GitHub Copilot let a single person build software that would have required a team before. No-code and low-code platforms allow non-technical founders to build and test products quickly. Cloud infrastructure from Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure is cheaper and easier to use than ever. The practical cost of starting a technology business has dropped dramatically. In 2025, the Y Combinator accelerator program — arguably the world's most prestigious startup school — accepted its first cohort that included a meaningful number of solo founders who had built their initial products entirely with AI assistance. This is a real signal of how the barriers to entry have changed.

Japan's own startup ecosystem is also growing, partly inspired by the US model. The Japanese government has set ambitious targets to increase the number of startups and the volume of venture investment. Tokyo is actively competing with Seoul and Singapore to attract founders and investors. Several large Japanese corporations — including Sony, NTT, and Recruit Holdings — have established venture arms or startup accelerators. The number of Japanese startups that raised Series A rounds (typically five to fifteen million dollars) grew by over 30 percent in 2025 compared to 2023, according to data cited by Nikkei Asia. This is still small by US standards, but the direction is clear.

The single most practical takeaway is this: the skills that make someone valuable in the startup world — clear communication, fast learning, comfort with uncertainty, and the ability to use new tools quickly — are also becoming more valuable inside large Japanese corporations. The boundary between "startup skills" and "corporate skills" is blurring. If you are early in your career, investing time in understanding how modern technology companies work, how venture capital thinks, and how AI tools are changing product development will pay dividends whether you stay in your current company or eventually start something of your own.

**Leverage**

*Noun / Verb | /ˈlev.ər.ɪdʒ/ | Origin: from the Old French word "levier," meaning a tool used to lift heavy objects with less effort*

**Definition:** As a noun, leverage means the power or advantage you gain by using something — money, information, or a position — to achieve a bigger result. As a verb, it means to use something you already have to get more out of a situation.

**In context:**
1. "Japan has significant leverage in trade talks because Toyota employs over 40,000 workers at US manufacturing plants."
2. "The startup used its early partnership with a major retailer to leverage its way into three new markets within one year."
3. "We can leverage our existing client relationships to expand the new service line without a large marketing budget."

**Why it matters:** "Leverage" appears constantly in business English — in strategy documents, investor presentations, and management meetings. It is one of those words that sounds technical but is actually simple once you know it. Understanding it helps you follow financial news, where it also refers specifically to using borrowed money to increase investment returns.
