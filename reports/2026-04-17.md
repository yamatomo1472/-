## Main Story: Japan's Economy at a Crossroads — Weak Yen, Trade Pressure, and the Push to Change

For most of the past two years, a weak yen has been the defining story of Japan's economy. In early 2026, the yen is trading around 148 to 150 per US dollar. That is stronger than the historic lows near 160 seen in mid-2024, but it is still far weaker than the 110 to 115 range that many Japanese importers and consumers consider comfortable. The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate to 0.5 percent in January 2025 — its highest level since 2008 — and then moved again to 0.75 percent in late 2025. But these cautious steps have not been enough to push the yen significantly higher, because the US Federal Reserve has kept its own interest rates at elevated levels. The gap between US and Japanese interest rates remains wide, and that gap keeps pulling money away from the yen and toward the dollar.

For Japanese consumers, this matters every time they go to the supermarket. Food import costs have risen sharply. Japan imports roughly 60 percent of its food by caloric value, according to the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries. Wheat, soybeans, and cooking oil are all priced in dollars on global markets. When the yen is weak, every kilogram of imported butter or every bag of imported wheat flour costs more in Japanese yen. The government has tried to cushion this through fuel subsidies and some food price controls, but these measures are expensive and cannot last forever. The Cabinet Office reported in early 2026 that household real income — income adjusted for inflation — fell slightly for the third consecutive year in 2025. That is a quiet but serious problem for domestic demand.

Corporate Japan tells a different story, at least on the surface. Toyota, Sony, and other major exporters benefit from a weak yen because their overseas profits look larger when converted back into yen. Toyota reported record operating profits in its fiscal year ending March 2025, driven partly by the currency effect. Nikkei Asia noted that many large manufacturers locked in favorable exchange rates through hedging contracts, giving them some protection from volatility. However, this good news for exporters does not automatically flow through to workers or smaller domestic companies. Wage growth in Japan reached around 3.1 percent in 2025, according to data from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. That sounds positive, but with inflation running above 3 percent for much of the year, real wage gains were minimal for many workers.

The second major pressure point is trade policy. The United States under the current administration has maintained an aggressive posture on trade, and Japan has not been immune. Washington pushed Tokyo in late 2025 to accept new terms on automotive imports and semiconductor equipment. Japanese automakers already face a complex situation — they are investing heavily in electric vehicles to meet both US and European market requirements, while also managing the transition costs at home. The trade talks, reported by Reuters and Bloomberg in March 2026, produced a partial agreement that reduced some tariff pressures on Japanese cars but required Japan to buy more US agricultural products. For a country already navigating food price inflation, importing more expensive American beef and wheat adds another layer of political and economic complexity.

There is also a strategic dimension that goes beyond price tags. Japan is in the middle of a serious rethink of its supply chains. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed how dependent Japanese manufacturers were on single-source suppliers, especially in China. Since then, the government's "economic security" policy framework — formalized under the Economic Security Promotion Act — has pushed companies to identify critical supply chains and develop alternatives. Semiconductors are the clearest example. TSMC, the Taiwanese chipmaker, opened its first Japan factory in Kumamoto in 2024 and is now building a second. Rapidus, the Japanese government-backed startup aiming to make leading-edge chips domestically, has set up facilities in Hokkaido with a target to begin trial production in 2027. METI, the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, is pouring significant public funding into both projects because Tokyo sees chip independence as a matter of national security, not just industrial policy.

What does all of this mean for an early-career Japanese professional? Several things stand out. First, industries connected to semiconductors, defense technology, and clean energy are receiving massive public and private investment right now. Job opportunities and career development chances in these sectors are growing. Second, the pressure on household budgets is real, and many workers are looking for ways to increase their income — a theme we cover in today's second story. Third, understanding the yen and interest rates is no longer just for economists. When the Bank of Japan changes its policy rate, it affects your mortgage if you own a home, the cost of doing business for your employer, and the price of the groceries you buy. Following the BOJ's quarterly Outlook Report and the Ministry of Finance's regular currency statements gives you a practical edge.

Japan's demographic challenge adds a long-term weight to all of these short-term pressures. The country's working-age population is shrinking. The government's own projections suggest Japan could lose 30 percent of its total population by 2070 if current birth rates continue. The Kishida and then Ishiba administrations have each announced policies to raise the birth rate and attract skilled foreign workers, but neither effort has yet shown clear results. More foreign workers arrived in Japan in 2025 than in any previous year, according to Justice Ministry data, but the numbers remain small relative to the scale of the labor shortage. Companies in construction, nursing care, logistics, and food service are especially stretched. This shortage is also an opportunity — workers with skills in technology, languages, or project management are finding that their leverage in salary negotiations is growing.

The overall picture is one of a country in genuine transition. Japan is not in crisis, but it is under real stress. The yen is weak, trade terms are uncertain, wages are barely keeping up with prices, and the long-term demographic math is difficult. At the same time, investment in strategic industries is accelerating, the corporate sector is more profitable than it has been in decades, and there is a growing awareness — among both companies and individuals — that the old ways of doing things are no longer enough. For the morning commuter reading this on the train, the most useful takeaway is this: the economy is rewarding people who actively build skills, stay informed, and look for opportunities on the edges of the main story.

## Second Story: The Side Business Boom — How Japanese Professionals Are Building a Second Income in 2026

Something is quietly changing in Japan's working culture. For decades, working a side job — known in Japanese as "fukugyou" — was either prohibited by most large employers or seen as a sign that someone was struggling. Today, that stigma is fading fast. A 2025 survey by Recruit Works Institute found that nearly 28 percent of full-time employees in Japan were running some form of side income activity, up from around 10 percent just five years earlier. The government itself has encouraged the shift, loosening guidelines around side work as part of its broader push to make the labor market more flexible and to help workers cope with stagnant real wages.

What is driving individual professionals to act? The weak yen and persistent inflation are a major push factor. When the price of your weekly grocery run rises by 15 percent over two years but your take-home pay grows by only 3 percent, finding a new income stream stops being a hobby and starts being a financial strategy. A second and equally important factor is digital infrastructure. Japan's freelance platforms have grown significantly. Lancers and Crowdworks, the two largest domestic platforms, both reported double-digit growth in registered users through 2025. English-language platforms like Upwork are also becoming more accessible to Japanese professionals as AI translation tools lower the language barrier for basic client communication.

The most accessible entry points for a business professional are skills they already use every day. Data analysis and Excel or spreadsheet work is in constant demand from small and medium-sized businesses that lack in-house staff for this kind of work. A professional who can clean data, build dashboards, or write clear reports has a sellable skill right now. Graphic design and presentation creation are similar — many Japanese SMEs need polished pitch decks or marketing materials but cannot afford a full-time designer. If you are already good at PowerPoint or Canva, that skill has market value outside your company.

Translation and English tutoring represent another strong category, especially for B1-B2 level English speakers who are actively improving. You do not need to be fluent to tutor a beginner or to translate internal business documents. In fact, someone who recently went through the learning process themselves often teaches more effectively because they remember what was confusing. Several working professionals in Tokyo have built small but steady income streams by offering one-on-one business English coaching through platforms like Cafetalk or directly via social media, charging between 3,000 and 6,000 yen per 60-minute session. At even two sessions a week, that adds up to over 300,000 yen a year in extra income.

For professionals with deeper expertise, consulting is a natural next step. Retired and senior executives have long acted as independent advisors, but younger professionals with niche knowledge are entering this space too. Someone who manages logistics for a manufacturer, for example, might advise a startup that is trying to set up its first supply chain. Someone who works in HR at a large corporation might advise a growing small company on how to structure its hiring process. Platforms like Visasq and Expertise Connect have built markets specifically for this kind of short-format expert consultation, and the sessions are typically paid at 10,000 to 30,000 yen per hour.

One practical issue that stops many people before they start is the question of taxes and legal compliance. In Japan, if your side income exceeds 200,000 yen per year, you are required to file a final tax return — a "kakutei shinkoku" — and declare that income. Many professionals worry this process is complicated, but in reality, using the National Tax Agency's free "e-Tax" online system, most simple returns take less than two hours. Keeping basic records of income and business-related expenses in a simple spreadsheet is all the preparation you need. Expenses related to your side business — such as the cost of a course you take to improve a relevant skill, software you buy for client work, or even a portion of your internet bill — can reduce your taxable income.

The biggest barrier, according to people who have done it, is not legal or technical — it is psychological. Starting feels risky because it is visible. You must put your work in front of strangers and ask them to pay for it. The easiest way through this barrier is to start extremely small. Offer one service to one person at a low price. Use that first paid experience to learn what clients actually need, improve your process, and build confidence. Most successful freelancers describe their first client as more valuable for the learning than for the money. Once you have completed real paid work, the second client is considerably easier to find, and the third easier still.

The broader context here connects directly to today's main story. Japan's labor market is changing in ways that reward people who do not depend on a single employer. The government's "new capitalism" agenda, which the Ishiba administration has continued, explicitly encourages individuals to invest in their own skills and build diverse income sources. The corporate world is also shifting — more companies now allow side work openly, because they recognize that employees who are learning and growing outside the office often bring new ideas back into it. Building a side income is not a distraction from your career. Done well, it accelerates it.

## 📖 Word of the Day

**Hedge**

*Verb / Noun | /hɛdʒ/ | Origin: from Old English "hegg," meaning a fence or boundary — the financial meaning developed from the idea of building a protective barrier around an asset.*

**Definition:** To hedge means to take an action that reduces the risk of a financial loss. A hedge is the action or position itself. In business, it often means making one investment or agreement to protect against a negative change in another.

**In context:**
1. "Toyota hedged its currency exposure by locking in exchange rates for the next 12 months, protecting its profits from yen volatility."
2. "The company decided to hedge against rising energy costs by signing a long-term fixed-price electricity contract with a renewable supplier."
3. "We should hedge our supply risk by sourcing the same component from two different vendors instead of just one."

**Why it matters:** The word "hedge" appears constantly in financial news, analyst reports, and business strategy discussions. Understanding it helps you follow conversations about how companies manage uncertainty — which is a core topic in corporate finance, procurement, and strategic planning.
