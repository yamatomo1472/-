## Main Story: Japan and the United States Race to Close a Trade Deal Before Pressure Becomes Crisis

The trade relationship between Japan and the United States has reached a critical point in 2026. Since US President Donald Trump reintroduced broad tariffs in early 2025 — including a baseline 10% tariff on most imports and higher rates on specific sectors — Japan has been working urgently to negotiate a deal that protects its key export industries. As of May 2026, talks are ongoing, and the outcome will shape Japan's economic direction for years to come.

Japan exports approximately $150 billion worth of goods to the United States every year. Cars and car parts make up the largest share, accounting for roughly 30% of total exports. Japanese automakers like Toyota, Honda, and Nissan have significant manufacturing operations in the US, but they also ship many vehicles directly from Japan. The Trump administration placed a 25% tariff on imported automobiles in early 2025, which immediately increased costs for Japanese brands. Toyota estimated that the tariffs could cost the company several billion dollars per year if no deal is reached. That number puts enormous pressure on Japan's trade negotiators to move quickly.

Japan's chief trade negotiator, Ryosei Akazawa, has held multiple rounds of talks with US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer since early 2025. The two sides have discussed a wide range of issues, including agricultural imports, digital trade rules, and currency policy. One of the most sensitive topics is US demands that Japan open its rice market further. Japan has historically protected its domestic rice farmers with high tariffs — sometimes over 700% on imported rice. Any significant change to that system is politically dangerous for the ruling Liberal Democratic Party, which depends on rural voters for support. Prime Minister Fumio Kishida's government, and later the new leadership that followed, has tried to show flexibility without completely giving away protections that farmers depend on.

The talks gained new urgency in early 2026. According to reporting from Nikkei Asia, Japan and the US reached a partial agreement in principle in late April 2026 on some non-automotive goods, but the biggest issues — cars, steel, and agriculture — remain unresolved. The US wants Japan to buy more American beef, wheat, and soybeans. Japan wants the US to lower or remove the 25% auto tariff. Neither side has moved enough to close the gap completely, and both governments are under domestic pressure to look tough in negotiations.

Beyond autos and agriculture, there is another dimension to the trade dispute that matters deeply for Japan's future: semiconductors and technology supply chains. The United States has been tightening controls on advanced chip exports to China since 2022, and it has pressured allies including Japan to align with those policies. Japan responded by implementing its own export controls on advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment in 2023, a move that affected major Japanese companies like Tokyo Electron. In 2025 and into 2026, this technology competition has continued to grow. Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan are all caught between their security alliance with the United States and their deep economic ties with China, which remains one of Japan's largest trading partners.

For Japan's economy overall, the external pressure comes at a complicated moment. The Bank of Japan raised its benchmark interest rate in 2024 and again in early 2025, moving away from its long-standing ultra-loose monetary policy. This shift strengthened the yen somewhat after a period of extreme weakness in 2023 and 2024, when the yen fell to levels not seen in decades against the dollar. A stronger yen helps consumers by lowering import prices, but it hurts exporters by making Japanese goods more expensive abroad. With tariff pressures already squeezing margins, some manufacturers are accelerating plans to shift more production to the United States or Southeast Asia.

The broader global context is important here. The International Monetary Fund revised its 2026 global growth forecast downward earlier this year, citing trade policy uncertainty as a key risk. Japan's own growth outlook has been modest — the IMF projected around 1% GDP growth for Japan in 2026. That is not a crisis number, but it leaves little room for error if a major shock like a full trade breakdown with the US occurs. Japan's government has passed supplementary budgets to support domestic demand, including subsidies for energy costs and support for small and medium-sized companies that are struggling with higher input costs.

One underreported part of this story is what the trade pressure is doing to Japan's corporate culture. Companies that relied on stable, long-term export relationships are now building more flexible supply chains. Large manufacturers are asking their Japanese suppliers to diversify and reduce dependence on any single market or country. This is forcing small and medium-sized companies to upgrade their operations faster than they planned, which creates both stress and opportunity. Companies that can adapt quickly — by adopting new technology, entering new markets, or finding new customers — will emerge stronger.

## Second Story: Why American Venture Capital Is Betting Big on the "Unsexy" Industries

For the past decade, venture capital in the United States meant one thing to most people: flashy consumer apps, social media platforms, and fast-growing software startups promising to change how we communicate. That era is not completely over, but it is no longer the center of the story. In 2025 and into 2026, the most interesting money in Silicon Valley — and beyond — is flowing into industries that most people would not call exciting. Think defense technology, nuclear energy, industrial automation, and agricultural biotech. This shift tells us something important about where the next wave of opportunity is coming from.

The numbers tell a clear story. According to data from PitchBook, a financial data company that tracks venture investments, defense technology startups in the US raised over $5 billion in 2025 — more than double the amount raised in 2022. Companies like Anduril Industries, which builds autonomous defense systems, have grown from small startups into companies valued at tens of billions of dollars. Shield AI, which develops AI-powered military aircraft systems, completed a major funding round in late 2025 at a valuation well above $5 billion. These are not companies building social media filters. They are companies solving some of the hardest engineering problems in the world, with government contracts and geopolitical demand driving their growth.

Nuclear energy is another area seeing a dramatic shift. After years of decline following the Fukushima disaster in 2011, nuclear power is experiencing a serious comeback — not the large traditional plants of the past, but smaller, modular reactors. Companies like Oklo and TerraPower in the US, backed by investors including Bill Gates, are working to build the next generation of reactors that are smaller, safer, and cheaper to build. In 2025, several of these companies secured both government support and private venture funding. The logic is simple: AI data centers require enormous amounts of electricity, and clean, reliable nuclear power is one of the few energy sources that can scale fast enough to meet that demand.

Industrial automation is another booming sector. American manufacturers have faced worker shortages and rising wages for years, and the answer for many companies is robots and automated systems. Startups in this space are not selling consumer gadgets — they are selling complex systems to factories, warehouses, and construction companies. Figure AI, which makes humanoid robots designed for factory work, raised significant funding in 2025 and began deploying its robots at partner manufacturing sites. The robotics sector as a whole attracted billions in venture investment last year, and the trend is continuing into 2026.

What is driving this shift away from consumer apps and toward these harder, more technical industries? Several factors are at work. First, the easy wins in consumer internet have already been taken. The major social media platforms, e-commerce sites, and streaming services already exist and are dominated by large incumbents. It is very hard to build the next Facebook or Amazon. Second, government spending and policy are pointing capital toward national security, clean energy, and domestic manufacturing. The US CHIPS Act, the Inflation Reduction Act, and increased defense budgets have created large pools of government money that smart startups can access alongside private venture capital. Third, AI tools have made it faster and cheaper to build complex technical products, lowering the barrier to entry in fields that used to require decades of expensive research.

For Japanese professionals, this shift has very direct implications. Japan already has world-class companies in many of these exact sectors — robotics, industrial equipment, nuclear technology, and advanced materials. Companies like Fanuc, Keyence, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, and Japan Steel Works are global leaders. The question is whether Japan's startup ecosystem can build on that industrial strength to create new growth companies, or whether American and Chinese startups will capture most of the value. Some promising signs exist. The Japanese government has made startup promotion a national priority, and venture investment in Japan has been growing steadily, though from a low base compared to the US.

The actionable insight for someone early in their career is this: the skills that matter most in this new wave of venture-backed startups are different from the skills needed for consumer app development. Deep engineering knowledge, understanding of regulatory environments, experience working with government agencies, and the ability to manage complex, long-cycle sales processes — these are the skills that companies in defense tech, nuclear, and industrial automation need most. If you work in or near any of these industries in Japan, you are sitting closer to a global opportunity than you might realize. Building connections with international investors, learning how US government procurement works, or simply staying informed about which technical problems American VCs are trying to solve — all of this is practical preparation for a world where these industries will attract serious capital and talent.

## 📖 Word of the Day

**Tariff**

*Noun | /ˈtær.ɪf/ | Origin: from Arabic "ta'rif," meaning notification or list of prices, which entered English through Italian and Spanish in the 16th century*

**Definition:** A tax that a government charges on goods coming into or leaving the country. Tariffs raise the price of imported goods, making domestic products relatively cheaper by comparison.

**In context:**
1. "The United States placed a 25% tariff on imported automobiles, which immediately raised the cost of Japanese cars sold in America."
2. "When tariffs on steel went up, the factory had to find a new supplier or accept higher production costs."
3. "Our team needs to recalculate the export pricing because the new tariff structure changes our margin significantly."

**Why it matters:** The word "tariff" appears in nearly every major business and economics news story in 2025 and 2026. Understanding exactly what a tariff is — and how it flows from government policy through to consumer prices and company profits — is essential for following global business news and for any professional involved in trade, finance, procurement, or strategy.
