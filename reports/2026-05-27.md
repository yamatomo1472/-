When oil prices rise, the cost of producing electricity, running factories, and transporting goods all go up. Strong demand from the clean energy sector — solar panels, wind turbines, and electric vehicle charging infrastructure all need large amounts of copper — has kept prices supported even as Chinese construction demand has softened. Aluminum prices have been more volatile, pulled between lower energy costs in some regions and continued high demand from the automotive and aerospace sectors. The practical takeaway from all of this is straightforward. Commodity prices affect almost every business, whether or not the business deals with raw materials directly. ## 📖 Word of the Day

**Tariff**

*Noun | /ˈtær.ɪf/ | Origin: from Arabic ta'rifa, meaning "notification" or "list of fees," which entered European languages through Spanish in the 16th century*

**Definition:** A tariff is a tax that a government places on goods that are imported from another country. It makes foreign products more expensive and is often used to protect domestic industries or as a tool in trade negotiations.

**In context:**
1. "The new 25 percent tariff on Japanese automobiles entering the United States has forced Toyota and Honda to reconsider where they build their vehicles."
2. "When tariffs rise, consumers often end up paying more at the store because importers pass the extra cost along to buyers."
3. "My manager asked me to calculate how the new tariff on steel imports would affect our production costs for the next quarter."

**Why it matters:** The word "tariff" appears almost daily in business and financial news, especially now that trade policy is one of the biggest forces shaping the global economy. Understanding the difference between a tariff, a quota, and a subsidy — three common trade tools — gives you a much clearer picture of the economic news you read every day.
