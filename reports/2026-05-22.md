## Main Story: Japan's Economy Under Pressure — The Yen, the BOJ, and What Comes Next

Japan began May 2026 in a difficult position. The yen fell to around 158 per US dollar in early May, its weakest point since mid-2024. The Bank of Japan held its benchmark interest rate steady at 0.5 percent at its April meeting, choosing caution over action. Meanwhile, the United States extended its tariff policy under the Trump administration, keeping a 10 percent base tariff on most Japanese goods and threatening sector-specific additions on pharmaceuticals and semiconductors. These three forces — a weak currency, a cautious central bank, and hostile trade policy — are now pressing on Japan's economy at the same time.

The yen's weakness is a double-edged story. On one side, Japanese exporters benefit. Toyota, Sony, and Hitachi all earn large portions of their revenue in dollars and euros. When those earnings are converted back to yen, the numbers look bigger. Toyota reported strong earnings for the fiscal year ending March 2026, partly because of this currency advantage. On the other side, Japanese consumers and businesses that import goods are being hurt. Energy costs remain high because Japan buys most of its oil and gas in dollars. Food prices have also risen. The Ministry of Agriculture reported that the price of wheat-based products rose by roughly 8 percent year-on-year as of March 2026, adding to household financial stress.

The Bank of Japan, led by Governor Kazuo Ueda, faces a genuinely hard choice. In January 2026, the BOJ raised rates from 0.25 percent to 0.5 percent — the highest level since 2008. That move was widely expected and the market absorbed it calmly. But the April decision to hold rates steady surprised some economists who had expected another increase. Governor Ueda explained that the BOJ was watching the impact of US tariffs on Japanese exports before taking further action. He used careful language, saying the bank would act "in a timely and appropriate manner" if conditions allowed. This kind of statement gives the BOJ flexibility, but it also signals that more rate hikes are not coming soon. Markets interpreted the hold as dovish — meaning supportive of a weaker yen — and the currency fell further after the announcement.

For context, the BOJ's hesitation makes sense when you understand Japan's history. For over two decades, Japan struggled with deflation — falling prices — which sounds good but is actually damaging because it causes consumers to delay purchases and businesses to delay investment. The BOJ spent years using ultra-low interest rates and massive bond purchases to fight deflation. Only recently has inflation returned to a more normal level. Core inflation in Japan reached 3.2 percent in March 2026, according to the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications. That is above the BOJ's 2 percent target. But Ueda and his colleagues want to make sure this inflation is sustainable before raising rates too quickly. They are aware that a premature hike could slow growth and destroy the progress made in recent years.

Trade is another major concern. The US-Japan trade relationship has always been important, but it has become more complicated under the current US administration. Japan exported approximately 150 billion dollars' worth of goods to the United States in 2025, with cars, auto parts, and electronics making up the largest share. The 10 percent US tariff, announced in April 2025, has already added pressure on Japanese automakers. Honda announced in March 2026 that it would accelerate its plans to shift more car production to North America to avoid tariff costs. Nissan, already struggling with a weak business performance and ongoing merger discussions with Honda, faces even more pressure. Japanese trade negotiators have been in Washington for multiple rounds of talks, but as of mid-May 2026, no new trade deal has been signed. Japan's Chief Cabinet Secretary confirmed that negotiations are continuing, but progress is slow.

On the global stage, Japan is also watching China carefully. The US and China reached a temporary 90-day tariff truce in mid-May 2026, reducing some tariffs significantly for that period. A more stable US-China relationship reduces some of the geopolitical risk in Asia. But a recovering Chinese economy could also mean stronger competition for Japanese manufacturers in global markets. Japan's strategic position — closely allied with the United States but deeply economically connected to China — makes every shift in that bilateral relationship important.

However, much of the wage increase has been absorbed by higher living costs, meaning real wage growth — adjusted for inflation — remains small. Workers are earning more on paper, but many feel no richer in practice.

For Japanese business professionals specifically, this environment has several direct implications. Japan's population fell by another 800,000 people in 2025 according to government statistics, continuing a trend that puts enormous pressure on public finances and labor supply. These structural challenges will not be solved quickly. But they are the backdrop against which every business decision in Japan is being made right now.

The next big moment to watch is the BOJ's June policy meeting, scheduled for mid-June. ## Second Story: How Japanese Professionals Are Building Side Businesses in the Age of Remote Work

Something significant is changing in how Japanese professionals think about their careers. The old model — join one company, stay loyal for decades, retire with a pension — is giving way to something new. More workers are building side businesses, taking on freelance clients, and creating independent income streams alongside their main jobs. This shift is happening faster than many expected, and it is creating real opportunities for people who are willing to act.

The numbers tell a clear story. A 2025 survey by Recruit Holdings found that approximately 14 percent of Japanese full-time employees reported having some form of side income — up from around 9 percent in 2022. The Japanese government has actively encouraged this trend. In 2022, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare updated its model employment rules to make it easier for employees to do side work without violating their contracts. Many large companies, including Panasonic, Fujitsu, and NTT, have updated their internal policies to allow side businesses under certain conditions, such as not competing directly with the employer and not working excessive hours. This policy environment has lowered one of the biggest barriers that used to stop people from starting.

The most successful side businesses tend to fall into a few clear categories. Translation and localization work is one of the most natural fits for Japanese professionals improving their English. A B1-B2 English speaker with deep expertise in a specific industry — finance, manufacturing, healthcare — can command strong rates from companies that need accurate, context-aware translation. Freelance translation platforms like Gengo and Conyac have active markets, and direct client relationships often pay even more. A professional with five or more years of industry experience can realistically earn 3,000 to 7,000 yen per hour for specialized translation work, according to data from the Japan Translation Federation.

Online consulting and advisory work is another growing area. Platforms like Coconala, which is Japan's largest skills marketplace, reported a 32 percent year-on-year increase in registered professional sellers in 2025. Categories growing fastest include career coaching, financial planning advice, marketing consulting, and business English coaching — which is particularly relevant for someone who is currently building their own English skills. A person who has already succeeded in improving their English and understands the Japanese business context can help others do the same thing, while earning extra income in the process.

Content creation and writing are increasingly viable, though they require more patience to build. Japanese professionals who write in English about their area of expertise — whether on a personal website, on LinkedIn, or through a newsletter platform like Substack — gradually build an audience that can lead to consulting inquiries, speaking opportunities, or product sales. The key insight here is specificity. A general blog about "business tips" gets lost in the crowd. A focused newsletter about, for example, "how Japanese automotive engineers think about supply chain risk" fills a gap that very few people can fill. This kind of content attracts a niche but highly engaged audience of readers in companies that value that specific knowledge.

The rise of AI tools has made starting a side business easier and less expensive than ever before. A professional can now use tools like ChatGPT or Claude to help draft client proposals, translate initial documents for proofreading, create content outlines, or build simple websites. This does not replace human expertise — clients pay for judgment and context, not just text — but it dramatically reduces the time cost of getting started. Many freelancers report that AI tools have doubled their productive output, allowing them to serve more clients without working more hours.

However, starting a side business in Japan requires understanding two practical issues. The first is taxes. If your side income exceeds 200,000 yen in a single year, you are required to file a final tax return — a kakutei shinkoku — with the National Tax Agency. Many first-time freelancers miss this requirement and face penalties later. Setting aside approximately 20 to 30 percent of side income for taxes from the beginning is a safe practice. The second issue is employer policy. While many companies now allow side work, you must check your specific employment contract. Some contracts still prohibit it entirely, and violating this rule can have serious consequences. Read the policy carefully and, if unclear, seek written confirmation from your HR department before starting.

The most important first step is simply to start small and test. Pick one skill you already have — writing, analysis, translation, teaching, project management — and offer it to one client or platform at a low price or even for free to build a track record. The goal in the first three months is not to earn money; it is to learn how the process works, what clients actually want, and how much time the work takes. Once you have that experience, you can raise your rates and become more selective. The professionals who succeed at building side businesses are not necessarily the most talented — they are the ones who started early, learned from feedback, and kept going.

## 📖 Word of the Day

**Headwinds**

*Noun (plural) | /ˈhɛd.wɪndz/ | Origin: from the literal wind blowing against the front of a moving aircraft or ship, first used as a financial metaphor in the early 20th century*

**Definition:** Conditions or forces that make progress more difficult or slow growth down. In business and economics, headwinds are challenges coming from the external environment — things like rising costs, weak demand, or unfavorable policy.

**In context:**
1. "Japan's export sector faces significant headwinds from the stronger dollar and new US tariffs on automotive goods."
2. "The CFO warned investors that currency headwinds would reduce reported earnings by approximately 5 percent this quarter."
3. "Our division is performing well despite the macroeconomic headwinds — I think our team's focus on cost control has made a real difference."

**Why it matters:** You will see "headwinds" — and its opposite, "tailwinds" — in almost every earnings report, economic briefing, and financial news article. Understanding this word helps you read business English faster and sound natural when discussing economic conditions with international colleagues.
