## Main Story: U.S. Tariffs, Yen Volatility, and the Reshaping of Japan's Export Economy

Japan's export-driven economy is facing one of its most complex environments in decades. The United States, Japan's second-largest trading partner after China, reimposed broad tariffs on manufactured goods in early 2026. The move came as part of a wider American policy push to protect domestic industry and reduce its trade deficit. For Japan, which exported roughly ¥20 trillion worth of goods to the U.S. in 2025, the timing is deeply uncomfortable.

The new U.S. tariffs target automobiles, auto parts, steel, and electronics — the exact sectors where Japan has the most exposure. Toyota, Honda, and Nissan together account for a large share of Japanese exports to North America. Under the new schedule, passenger vehicles face a 25 percent tariff when entering the United States. That number, if maintained, would add thousands of dollars to the cost of each car sold. Automakers are already running the numbers, and the results are not encouraging. Nikkei Asia reported in March 2026 that Toyota was reviewing its production allocation between its U.S. plants and its Japanese facilities, with a decision expected by summer.

The yen adds another layer of difficulty. After years of ultra-low interest rates, the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 0.75 percent in January 2026, the highest level since 2008. This pushed the yen stronger, moving from around ¥155 per dollar in mid-2025 toward ¥142 by late March 2026. A stronger yen is bad for exporters. When Japanese companies bring their dollar-denominated overseas profits back to Japan, they receive fewer yen. A shift from ¥155 to ¥142 per dollar can reduce the yen value of overseas earnings by 8 to 10 percent. Combined with higher U.S. tariffs, some Japanese exporters now face a double squeeze on their margins.

The Bank of Japan's shift in policy is itself significant. Governor Kazuo Ueda has signaled that the central bank will move carefully but will continue normalizing rates if wages and inflation stay on track. Japan's core inflation rate held at 2.8 percent in February 2026, according to data from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications. Wage growth, long the missing piece in Japan's economic recovery, rose 3.1 percent on average in the 2025 spring labor negotiations, the highest increase in over 30 years. These signals tell the BOJ that the economy can handle higher rates. But they also mean the yen is likely to stay stronger than it was in 2023 and 2024, keeping pressure on exporters.

Not all the news is negative. Japan has been quietly building new trade relationships that could reduce its dependence on the U.S. market. The Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, known as the CPTPP, has expanded. The UK joined formally in late 2024, and negotiations with other economies are ongoing. Japan is also deepening trade ties through its Economic Partnership Agreements with the European Union and the UK. These agreements reduce tariffs on Japanese goods and open new markets for services, investment, and digital trade. Bloomberg reported in February 2026 that Japanese machinery exports to CPTPP countries grew 7 percent year-on-year in 2025, partly offsetting slower growth in the U.S.

Supply chain restructuring is the other major story running beneath the headline numbers. Since the pandemic, and accelerating through the U.S.-China tech war, companies have been rethinking where they make things. The phrase "China Plus One" became common in business circles around 2022. It describes the strategy of keeping some production in China while also building capacity in a second country — usually Vietnam, India, Thailand, or Mexico. Japan's own manufacturers have followed this path. Toyota has expanded production in Thailand. Sony has shifted some camera manufacturing to Malaysia. Panasonic has invested heavily in India for its consumer electronics line.

India deserves special attention. Japan has become one of India's largest foreign investors, and the relationship is strengthening. Prime Minister Modi's "Make in India" initiative has made it easier for foreign companies to set up factories. Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, known as METI, published a report in January 2026 identifying India as a strategic priority for Japanese manufacturers looking to diversify away from China. For Japanese professionals in manufacturing, logistics, or finance, building knowledge of the India market is no longer optional — it is increasingly a career advantage.

The semiconductor sector is a window into the future of Japan's industrial strategy. The government-backed company Rapidus is building a next-generation chip factory in Hokkaido, aiming to produce 2-nanometer chips by 2027. The project has attracted over ¥3.3 trillion in public and private funding. TSMC, the Taiwanese chip giant, opened its first Japanese plant in Kumamoto in early 2024 and has already announced a second Kumamoto facility. These investments are transforming Japan from a country that lost its semiconductor leadership in the 1990s into a serious player in the global chip supply chain. Reuters reported in March 2026 that TSMC's first Kumamoto plant was running at over 90 percent capacity, well ahead of schedule.

What does all of this mean for you, as a Japanese business professional? Second, supply chain diversification is creating real job opportunities in countries like India, Vietnam, and Thailand. Japanese companies need people who can work across cultures and understand international logistics. Third, the shift in BOJ policy and the stronger yen will affect corporate earnings, budgeting, and financial planning in ways that will show up in your company's strategy meetings over the next year. An agreement that carves out exemptions for Japanese automakers would be a significant positive for the market. But even without that, Japanese companies are already adapting — through local production, new partnerships, and geographic diversification. The companies that move fastest will gain the most.

## Second Story: How Japanese Professionals Are Building Side Businesses in 2026

The concept of a side business is no longer unusual in Japan. For decades, most large Japanese companies had strict rules against employees doing outside paid work. That is changing fast. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare updated its model employment guidelines in 2022 to make side work easier, and many major employers — including Panasonic, Hitachi, and Fujitsu — have since removed or relaxed their bans. In 2026, having a freelance income stream alongside your main job is not just acceptable; for many early-career professionals, it is becoming a smart financial strategy.

The numbers support the trend. A survey by Lancers, Japan's largest freelance platform, found that the total value of the freelance economy in Japan reached ¥28 trillion in 2025. The number of people doing some form of freelance work alongside a full-time job grew to approximately 8 million, up from 5.7 million in 2021. These are not all people building businesses. Many are doing small projects on evenings and weekends. But the direction is clear: more Japanese workers are testing the market for their skills outside their main employer.

What kinds of side work are growing fastest? Three areas stand out right now. The first is digital content and translation. Japan has a massive demand for bilingual professionals who can localize English content into Japanese — and vice versa. Companies launching products in Japan need accurate, natural-sounding Japanese copy. Technical translation, especially in legal, medical, and IT fields, pays particularly well. If you are reading this newsletter, your English ability alone puts you ahead of most candidates for this kind of work. Rates for professional translation typically range from ¥3,000 to ¥7,000 per 1,000 Japanese characters, and experienced translators working steadily can earn ¥100,000 to ¥200,000 per month on the side.

The second growth area is B2B consulting and advisory work for foreign companies entering Japan. Japan remains one of the world's most attractive consumer markets, but it is notoriously difficult to enter without local knowledge. Foreign companies — especially American and European startups — are willing to pay for guidance on everything from retail distribution to regulatory compliance. A Japanese professional with even three to five years of experience in a specific industry has valuable knowledge that these companies simply cannot get elsewhere. Platforms like Visasq and Expert Connect have made it easy to register as a paid expert and take one-hour paid consultation calls. Fees typically range from ¥15,000 to ¥50,000 per hour depending on your background.

The third area is online education and coaching. The global market for online learning grew to over $350 billion in 2025, according to data from HolonIQ. Japan-specific courses — teaching Japanese language, Japanese business culture, or Japan-based professional skills — sell well on platforms like Udemy, Teachable, and Skillshare. A mid-career professional who understands how Japanese corporate culture works, for example, can build a course aimed at foreign managers moving to Japan. Beyond courses, coaching and mentoring services for people earlier in their careers are also in demand. Video coaching via Zoom, charged at an hourly rate, requires almost no upfront investment to start.

Starting a side business in Japan in 2026 is more practical than it was five years ago, but there are real steps to follow. The first step is to check your employment contract. Many companies that have relaxed their rules still require you to notify HR before starting outside paid work. Skipping this step can create legal and professional problems. The second step is to register as a sole proprietor, or kojin jigyo-nushi, with your local tax office. This is free and straightforward. Once registered, you can deduct business expenses — your laptop, internet connection, professional subscriptions — from your taxable income.

The tax side of side income deserves attention. In Japan, if your side income exceeds ¥200,000 in a year, you must file a final tax return, known as a kakutei shinkoku. Many people doing small amounts of freelance work do not realize this until they receive a notice from the tax office. Getting ahead of this early — opening a separate bank account for side income, keeping records of expenses, and filing your first return yourself or with an accountant — will save you stress later. The National Tax Agency website has English-language guidance, and several online services like freee and MoneyForward can make the process much simpler.

The most important thing is to start small and specific. Do not try to build a full business on the first weekend. Instead, pick one skill you already use at work, find one type of client who needs that skill, and complete one paid project. That first paid project — even if it is small — proves to you that the market exists. From there, you can decide how much time and energy to invest. Many professionals find that just two to three small projects per month are enough to add ¥50,000 to ¥100,000 to their monthly income without significantly affecting their main job performance.

**The Rise of Parametric Insurance as a Risk Tool for Business**

Traditional insurance pays out after a company proves it suffered a loss. That process takes time and often leads to disputes. Parametric insurance works differently — it pays automatically when a specific event occurs, such as a typhoon reaching a certain wind speed or rainfall exceeding a defined level. For Japanese businesses exposed to natural disaster risk, this is a faster and more reliable form of protection. The global parametric insurance market grew to $15 billion in 2025, according to Swiss Re, and adoption in Asia is accelerating. Risk managers in Japan should understand this product as part of a broader resilience strategy.

## 📖 Word of the Day

**Hedge**

*Verb / Noun | /hɛdʒ/ | Origin: from Old English "hege," meaning a fence or boundary — later used in finance to mean protecting yourself on both sides of a risk*

**Definition:** To hedge means to take action that reduces your risk of loss from a future event. In finance, a hedge is a specific investment or contract that offsets potential losses in another position.

**In context:**
1. "Toyota uses currency hedging contracts to protect its earnings from sudden moves in the yen-dollar exchange rate."
2. "The CFO told investors the company had hedged 70 percent of its U.S. dollar exposure for the next fiscal year."
3. "We should hedge our reliance on one supplier by developing at least two backup vendors before the busy season."

**Why it matters:** "Hedge" appears constantly in financial news, earnings reports, and corporate strategy documents. Understanding it clearly — both as a verb describing protective action and as a noun describing the instrument used — helps you follow discussions about risk management, foreign exchange, and corporate finance with much greater confidence.
