## Main Story: Japan Navigates Tariff Pressure, a Weak Yen, and Rising Rates — What It Means for Your Career and Company

Japan entered 2026 under serious economic pressure from multiple directions at once. The United States, under President Donald Trump's second administration, reimposed broad tariffs on imports from dozens of countries in early 2025. Japan was hit with a baseline tariff of 24 percent on most goods, though a temporary 90-day pause brought many rates down to 10 percent while negotiations continued. That pause did not remove the threat. As of April 2026, Japan and the US are still in active trade talks, and the final outcome remains uncertain. Japanese exporters, policymakers, and business leaders are watching these talks with intense focus.

The industries most exposed to US tariffs are the ones that define Japan's export economy. Automobiles are at the top of the list. Japanese automakers — Toyota, Honda, Nissan, and Subaru — sell millions of cars to American consumers every year. A 25 percent tariff on imported vehicles, which Trump announced in late March 2025, directly raises the price of Japanese cars in the US market. Toyota alone produces roughly 1.5 to 2 million vehicles for the American market annually. When those cars become more expensive, American buyers shift toward domestic brands. That means lower sales, lower revenue, and eventually, pressure to cut costs or move production. Toyota has already signaled it is reviewing its supply chain strategy, and Honda has accelerated plans to expand US manufacturing capacity to reduce tariff exposure.

Steel and aluminum face separate tariffs of 25 percent, and electronics goods face additional scrutiny. Japanese firms like Panasonic, Sony, and Murata Manufacturing have complex supply chains that cross multiple borders. A tariff on goods made in one country can ripple through to components made in Japan. No major Japanese manufacturer is completely insulated from these changes. The Japan External Trade Organization, known as JETRO, estimated in early 2026 that the combined tariff impact could reduce Japan's GDP growth by 0.3 to 0.5 percentage points if current rates remain in place through the end of the year.

While the tariff situation creates problems for exporters, the weak yen creates a more complicated picture. The yen has been trading at levels between 145 and 155 to the US dollar for much of the past year. A weak yen means Japanese products are relatively cheap in dollar terms, which gives exporters a price advantage. In that narrow sense, the yen's weakness partially offsets the tariff burden. But a weak yen is also painful for Japanese companies and households in other ways. Japan imports almost all of its energy — oil, gas, and coal — priced in dollars. It also imports large amounts of food, semiconductors, and industrial materials. When the yen is weak, all of these imports become more expensive in yen terms. Consumer prices in Japan rose about 3 percent year-on-year in early 2026, according to data from the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications. For workers whose wages have not kept pace with prices, this is a real reduction in purchasing power.

The Bank of Japan responded to this environment with a historic policy change. After years of ultra-low interest rates and massive bond purchases — a policy called yield curve control — the BOJ began raising its benchmark rate in 2024. By early 2026, the policy rate had reached 0.75 percent, its highest level in roughly fifteen years. Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda has described this shift as a move toward normalization, meaning the BOJ believes Japan's economy is finally strong enough to handle higher rates. The logic is that sustained wage growth and steady inflation, after decades of deflation, give the BOJ room to act. The central bank has been watching the annual spring wage negotiations, known as shunto, very closely. In March 2026, Japan's largest companies agreed to wage increases averaging around 5 percent — the second consecutive year of strong wage growth. The BOJ sees this as a sign that a positive cycle between wages and prices is taking hold.

Higher interest rates from the BOJ have global consequences, not just domestic ones. For years, investors borrowed cheaply in yen and invested in higher-yielding assets in the US and elsewhere — a strategy called the carry trade. When the BOJ raises rates, the cost of those yen loans goes up. That forces some investors to sell their foreign assets and buy back yen to repay their loans. This is one reason why yen movements can trigger sharp volatility in global financial markets, as happened in August 2024 when a BOJ rate hike helped spark a sudden stock market sell-off. As the BOJ continues its path toward normalization in 2026, financial professionals in Japan need to understand that domestic policy decisions now have a direct link to global market swings.

For Japanese business professionals, all of this creates both challenges and opportunities. On the challenge side, companies that rely heavily on US exports will face ongoing pressure to rethink their supply chains. Many are accelerating their shift toward Southeast Asia, India, and Europe to diversify away from dependence on a single market. Japanese investment in Vietnam, Thailand, and India has grown steadily, and firms see these regions as both manufacturing bases and growing consumer markets. On the opportunity side, the weak yen and Japan's improved wage picture are attracting more foreign investment into Japan. Foreign direct investment hit a record high in fiscal 2025, according to JETRO data. Foreign firms see Japan as a stable, high-quality market where labor costs, in dollar terms, are now quite competitive.

Prime Minister Fumio Kishida stepped down in late 2024, and his successor has continued a broadly pro-growth agenda focused on digital transformation, green energy investment, and encouraging startups. The government's GX, or Green Transformation, plan commits over 20 trillion yen to decarbonization over the next decade. That is creating new work in clean energy, infrastructure, and consulting. The semiconductor strategy, backed by a partnership with Taiwan's TSMC and US chipmaker Micron, is bringing new factories and thousands of jobs to Kumamoto and Hokkaido.

Skills in English — exactly what you are building right now — are increasingly valuable as Japanese firms hire more international staff and form more cross-border partnerships. If your company is expanding into Southeast Asia or discussing supply chain shifts, you are now closer to the center of Japan's most important economic debates. Staying informed is not a hobby. It is a professional advantage.

While Japan navigates trade pressures and policy shifts at home, one region is pulling ahead as the world's most exciting growth story: Southeast Asia. The ten countries of ASEAN — including Indonesia, Vietnam, the Philippines, Thailand, and Malaysia — are home to nearly 700 million people. More importantly for business professionals, the region's middle class is growing at a pace that no other part of the world can match right now. For Japanese companies and individual professionals alike, Southeast Asia represents one of the clearest business opportunities of this decade.

Indonesia is the biggest single opportunity. With a population of over 280 million people, Indonesia is the fourth most populous country in the world. Its economy grew at around 5 percent in 2025, driven by strong domestic consumption, a young workforce, and a government committed to infrastructure investment. Jakarta is shifting its role as the administrative capital to the new city of Nusantara in Borneo, and that massive construction project alone is generating billions in business for engineering, logistics, and materials companies. Japanese firms like Sumitomo Corporation and Mitsui are already deeply involved in Indonesian infrastructure. Japanese banks, including MUFG and SMBC, have made large equity investments in Indonesian financial institutions to gain access to the country's fast-growing loan and payment markets.

Vietnam is the manufacturing story of the moment. When US tariffs hit China hard, many global companies accelerated their strategy to move production out of China and into Vietnam. Apple now assembles a growing share of its AirPods and iPads there. Samsung produces a large portion of its smartphones in Vietnamese factories. Japanese electronics and auto parts suppliers have followed their customers, setting up plants in industrial zones near Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City. Vietnam's exports grew at double-digit rates through much of 2025. The challenge now is that Vietnam itself faces US tariff scrutiny — the country received a 46 percent tariff rate in early 2025 before the 90-day pause took effect. If those tariffs return at full force, Vietnam's export advantage will shrink and companies will need to look further, possibly toward Indonesia, India, or Mexico.

The Philippines is emerging as a major destination for business process outsourcing and digital services. English fluency is very high in the Philippines, and labor costs remain low compared to Northeast Asia. Global companies have long used Manila as a hub for customer service and back-office work. Now, with AI tools handling routine tasks, the work moving to the Philippines is becoming more complex — think data analysis, software development, and financial reporting. This shift is creating a growing market for Japanese companies that sell software tools, training services, or professional consulting to rapidly expanding Philippine businesses.

For Japanese professionals looking to grow their careers, Southeast Asia knowledge is becoming a key differentiator. Companies that are expanding into the region need people who understand local business culture, can work with regional partners, and can communicate in English — because English, not Japanese, is the working language across ASEAN. If you are in finance, you should understand how capital flows between Japan and ASEAN markets. If you are in sales or marketing, knowing how young Southeast Asian consumers behave differently from Japanese consumers could make you invaluable to your company's regional team. If you are in operations or supply chain, the ability to map a multi-country production network is a skill that is in high demand right now.

**Normalization**

*Noun | /ˌnɔːr.mə.lɪˈzeɪ.ʃən/ | Origin: from the Latin "norma," meaning a rule or standard, combined with the English suffix "-ization," indicating a process*

**Definition:** In economics and finance, normalization means returning a policy or condition to its usual or expected level after a period of unusual change. Central banks use this word to describe the process of raising interest rates back toward normal levels after keeping them unusually low.
